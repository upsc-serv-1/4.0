**Question:** Describe the appointment process, duties, and functions of the Attorney General of India. Also, state  the restrictions governing the functioning of the office.

#### Answer from ForumIAS

| **Approach:** • Introduce the answer by giving a brief background about the post of Attorney General of India. <br>• In the body, describe the appointment process, duties and functions of the Attorney General of India. <br>• Next, state the restricting governing the functioning of the office. <br>• Conclude the answer by impressing on the fact that despite being a part of the Union Executive, the Attorney General is not a government servant. |
| --- |

**Article 76** of the Constitution creates the **office of Attorney General for India**, who is the **highest law officer** in the country. **Appointed by the President,** the AGI must be **qualified to become a judge of the Supreme** **Court**. She holds office **during the pleasure of the President.** As a **convention**, AGI **submits her resignation** **to the President** upon the **change of government**.

As the **chief law officer of the Government of India,** the Attorney General has been assigned the following **duties and functions**:
1. a) Advise the government** on **legal matters** referred by the **President (**advice on grant of full GST compensation to states). Performs **duties of a legal character** that are assigned to her by the **President.****

2. a) Appear **on behalf of the Union Government** in all cases in the **Supreme Court** where the Government of India is a party (e.g., ***Anuradha Bhasin case 2020***);
   b) represent the Union Government in **High Courts,** when required.

3. a) Represents the Government of India in **any reference** made by the **President** to the **Supreme Court** under **Article 143 (**Presidential reference in 2G case);
   b) discharge the **functions** conferred on her by the **Constitution or any law (**Contempt of Courts Act 1971).

4. a) Participate in parliamentary proceedings** as and when required, without any **voting rights;
   b) **assist the Court** in the interest of **rightful administration of justice (**Taj Corridor case).

However, certain **limitations** have been placed in the office:
1. a) The Attorney General is **disallowed** to advise or argue **cases against the Union Government;**
   b) she must not **advise or argue** in cases where she is supposed to advise **for the Union Government.**

2. a) She must not **defend accused persons in criminal proceedings** without the **permission of the** **Union Government;**
   b) to prevent **conflict of interests,** she must not accept **appointment as a** **director in any company** without government permission.

3. a) The Attorney General must refrain from **advising any government body** unless the **proposal** or a reference in this regard is **received through the Ministry of Law and Justice**.

Despite being a **member of the Union Executive,** the Attorney General of India **does not fall** under the category of **government servants.** As a result, **neither** is she a **full-time legal counsel** for the Union Government **nor** is she **debarred from private practice.** ***(351 words)***

**Q.****2) Reorganization of States remains a parliamentary prerogative in India, with the Constitution assigning** **only a consultative role to State legislatures. Examine.**

***Approach: Introduce the answer with articles of the Constitution dealing with reorganization of states. In*** ***the body of answer, examine the factors due to which the reorganization of states remains a parliamentary*** ***prerogative in India, with the Constitution assigning only a consultative role to the State legislatures.*** ***Conclude by highlighting the constitutional safeguards in place to protect the interests of the state.***

**Article 2** of the Constitution deals with **admission or establishment of new states** while **Article 3** deals **with** **alteration** of areas, **boundaries or names** of existing states. These provisions establish India as an **indestructible union of destructible states**, a feature of **federal state** with **unitary bias.**

While the **Constitution provides substantial power to State Assemblies**, reorganization of states remains a parliamentary prerogative in India. The State legislatures have been assigned only a consultative role in the process due to the following factors:
1. While the **drafting of the Constitution**, India was emerging from colonial **rule.** Given the challenges of a **nascent democracy** and the **recent partition**, constitution-makers favoured **centralized control** over **state reorganization.** They believed it was crucial to prevent **balkanization** and preserve the unity **and integrity** of India.

2. After independence, **existing boundaries** were seen as **arbitrary remnants of colonial rule**. **Redrawing boundaries** was recognized as **necessary** due to this reason. However, involving the states directly risked **prioritizing regional over national interests**. Parliamentary control ensured balanced decision-making for **national cohesion and stability.** E.g., Patiala and East Punjab States Union **(PEPSU) merged into Punjab** state in 1956.

3. **Administrative convenience** and **practicality** were also important considerations. Parliamentary control over the matter ensures a **standardized process** across the country. It provides **consistency** in dealing with **demands for new states or** alterations in state boundaries. Also, **regional aspirations** have been **accommodated by the Parliament** from time to time. E.g., reorganization of NE states, creation of **Telangana (2014)**.

Nevertheless, the framers of the Constitution put **safeguards in place** to protect the interest of the **affected** **states**. Any bill for the reorganisation of the states requires **prior recommendation** of the President. The concerned State Assembly has to be **consulted** by the President and the bill must also be **scrutinized and passed** by the **Council of States**.***(297 words)***

| **Approach:** • Introduce the answer by giving a brief background of the Tenth Schedule of the Constitution. <br>• In the body, first highlight its successes. <br>• Next, point out how structural weaknesses and implementation challenges continue to undermine its effectiveness. <br>• the ways in which it has failed to achieve its objectives. <br>• Conclude with suggestions to reform the law. |
| --- |

**Tenth Schedule of the Constitution**, also known as the **anti-defection law**, was inserted via the **52****nd** **Constitutional Amendment (1985).** It contains provisions to **disqualify legislators** from their membership on **grounds of defection**.

It has played an important role in **safeguarding the foundations of democracy** in the following ways:
1. The law has **strengthened democratic stability** by reducing **individual defections**. This has led to stable governments and **fewer premature elections** at both central and state levels.

2. The disqualification provision **bars the defectors** from **ministerial positions**. This has effectively reduced **opportunistic party-switching**(Aaya Ram Gaya Ram) driven by the **'lure of office’.**

3. The legislation has improved **party discipline** and **organizational cohesion** through strong **disincentives** against **arbitrary defections**.

4. Ensured respect for **popular mandate** which is often linked to a candidate’s **party affiliation** or **independent status.**

5. The **constitutional framework** established by the law provides a **clear mechanism (**e.g. grounds and procedure for disqualification) for addressing political defections.

Despite these positive outcomes, **structural weaknesses** and **implementation challenges**  continue to **undermine the law's effectiveness**, as highlighted in the Law Commission's 170th report:
1. a) **Loopholes** in the law (disqualification ceases to be effective on re-election) have been **exploited** to **manufacture mass resignations** (Karnataka) and **destabilize governments** (Madhya Pradesh, Uttarakhand);
   b) defections have been engineered to **deplete the ranks** of Opposition in the legislature (West Bengal).

2. **Practical challenges** have arisen because of the **Speaker's role** as the **adjudicating authority**. Courts have raised question marks on the **neutrality of the Speaker** (*Keisham Meghchandra Singh* *case 2020*) and **delays in deciding** disqualification petitions (*Maharashtra Speaker case 2020*).

3. a) **Exemption** granted to **merger** of 2/3rd members has been maneuvered to **serve political interests** (Goa);
   b) The law does not cover **activities outside** the legislature which are contrary to the **spirit of** **the law** (Jignesh Mevani case 2022).

4. a) Party leadership** has been **disproportionately strengthened** through the power to issue **whips**. This has come at the cost of **autonomy** of individual legislators (restricted freedom of speech and conscience) and **intra-party democracy** (can be used to curb dissent);**
   b) **ambiguous phrases** (“voluntarily gives up membership”) have resulted in **inconsistent interpretation** and **application**.

The recommendations of the Halim Committee, NCRWC, and Supreme Court judgments calling for **independent tribunals** and **fixed timelines for decisions** provide a **roadmap for reform**. These **institutional** **changes**, coupled with strengthening **intra-party democracy**, would help realize the **law's original objectives**. (***388 words***)

Tribunals are **quasi-judicial bodies** established to adjudicate specific **administrative matters** outlined in **Article 323A**, or **other designated areas (**e.g. taxation, industrial disputes) as specified in **Article 323B.** Tribunals have great **salience** in the Indian justice system, as discussed below:
1. a) Facilitate **speedier disposal** of cases (informal procedures- e.g. guided by principles of natural justice), thereby helping reduce **judicial backlog** (e.g. CAT);
   b) non-adversarial nature of proceedings** enables more consensual disposal of complicated cases (e.g. NCLT).**

2. a) Bring **domain knowledge** through integration of domain experts, thereby enabling **specialized** **adjudication** (e.g. ITAT);
   b) provide avenues for **low-cost litigation,** especially to the weaker sections of the society (e.g. Maintenance Tribunals under the Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007).

3. a) Enhance **access to justice** in far-fetched corners of the country (e.g. through regional benches);
   b) pioneer **progressive jurisprudence** in emerging areas (e.g. environmental justice- NGT)

However, tribunals’ functioning is **beset with multiple challenges:**
1. a) **Executive interference** in functioning (e.g. in appointment of chairpersons/members) violates the principle of **separation of powers;**
   b) **structural issues** with the tribunals (vacancies, inadequate support staff, infrastructural gaps) reduce their functionality.

2. a) Diversity in **composition, structure and statutes** has created confusion about their powers, jurisdiction and functions, leading to **tribunalization of justice;**
   b) frequent appeals** against their decisions end up **prolonging** the judicial process, increasing **financial burden** on the parties and causing  **judicial delays.****

**Tribunal Reform Act 2021** rationalized the **number of tribunals** to address some of these concerns. However, to ensure these bodies **fulfill their intended mandate**, these reforms must be complemented with the suggestions of the Supreme Court (**judicial impact assessment, National Tribunals Commission)**.  ***(302*** ***Words)***

The office of Vice President (VP) is the **second-highest constitutional office** after the president. It is **modelled** on the lines of the **American Vice-President**. **Article 64** of the Constitution states that the VP shall also be the **ex-officio Chairperson of the Rajya Sabha**.

The **dual responsibility** encompasses a range of significant functions:
1. a) Ensures orderly conduct of House proceedings through **procedural oversight** (allocation of time for debates and discussions);
   b) provides **administrative supervision** over the functioning of Rajya Sabha Secretariat;
   c) **refers bills** to parliamentary committees based on subject matter expertise.

2. a) Maintains **institutional autonomy** of Rajya Sabha through **independent interpretation** of Rules of Procedure (Chairman's rulings become precedents for future reference);
   b) maintains decorum through **disciplinary powers** (can force a withdrawal of members; recommend them for suspension);
   c) casts a **deciding vote in case of a tie**, ensuring legislative impartiality (Article 100).

3. a) Protects **privileges** and rights of house members (Article 105);
   b) ensures **equitable participation** across political parties (proportional time allocation based on party strength);
   c) facilitates **consensus-building** on contentious issues (through all-party meetings and informal consultations).

4. a) Maintains **balance** between government business and agenda of individual legislators (can shift private members’ bills to any other day if there is no sitting on a Friday);
   b) upholds **constitutional** **provisions** regarding legislative procedures (Articles 107-111);c) **moderates debates** with impartiality ;
   d) adapts house procedures to **emerging challenges** (facilitated a hybrid model during Covid-19).

A **notice** for the motion of **removal** against the Rajya Sabha Chairman was given in December 2024. It has put the spotlight on the **removal procedure** for the Vice-President. The **procedure i**s as follows:
1. It requires a **resolution** to be introduced **only in Rajya Sabha** with minimum **14 days' notice (Article** **67(b))**.

2. Removal proceedings are not **presided by the Chairman**, following the principle of **natural justice** (nemo judex in causa sua).

3. The resolution for removal needs **approval** by a **majority of the then** membership (effective majority) of Rajya Sabha. Further, the **Lok Sabha** must also **agree** (simple majority) to such a resolution.

4. The procedure differs from Presidential impeachment as **no specific grounds** are mentioned in the Constitution (Article 67 vs Article 61).

Though the Vice-President **does not have executive powers**, the office remains **crucial for legislative stability** and political continuity, particularly in **presiding over Rajya Sabha** and assuming the **President’s role in case** **of vacancy**. ***(391 words)***

   a) **Permanent and regular committees,** which are **re-constituted from** **time to time;**
   b) three types of committees come under this category - **Financial Committees (**Public Accounts Committee**), Departmentally related Standing Committees (**Committee on Commerce) and **Other Parliamentary Standing Committees (**Business Advisory Committee).

2. **Ad hoc Committees:**
   a) Appointed for a **specific purpose** and **cease to exist** when the task is **finished;**
   b) divided into two categories- **Inquiry Committees (**JPC on 2G spectrum case) and **Advisory Committees (**JPC on Waqf Bill).

**Departmentally Related Standing Committees** (DRSCs) cover under their **jurisdiction** all the **Ministries/** **Departments of the Government of India** and comprise **members** from both the **Lok Sabha (21)** and the **Rajya** **Sabha (10).** They have played a crucial role in the **institutionalization of Parliament** in the following ways:
1. a) Consider the **demand for grants** of various ministries, thus aiding **effective debate over the** **Budget**;
   b) **examine** in-depth the **bills** referred to them, analyze **policies** and provide their **suggestions to strengthen** the same.

2. a) Provisions of **anti-defection laws** are **not applicable** to Committee proceedings. This allows individual **MPs to freely voice their opinions**;
   b) **closed-door nature of proceedings** enables members to **diverge from party line** and r**each consensus** on critical matters.

3. a) Provide a platform for **domain experts** to consult with public representatives and provide their inputs in policy making;
   b) reflect the principles of **direct democracy** by engaging with **grassroots** **organizations, civil society groups** and the **general public**.

4. a) Help make up for the l**oss of time** (disruptions, adjournments) on the **floor of the House**;
   b) **continuous nature of the functioning** of Committees compensates for the **steady decline in the** **sittings of the Parliament** (average annual sitting of the 17th Lok Sabha-55 days).

5. a) Enable the **Rajya Sabha** in exercising **financial control** over the Executive indirectly;
   b) facilitate **intellectual development of individual MPs** who can focus on particular policy domains and build expertise therein;
   c) make space for **opposition** to contribute to **government functioning**.

However, the following **factors** have impeded the ability of the DRSCs in this regard:
1. a) **Decrease** in the number of bills being referred to the Committees (only 16% in the 17th Lok Sabha);
   b) **non-binding** nature of the **recommendations** of the Committee.

2. a) Decline** in the working of the Committees (delays in submitting reports, lesser number of sittings for specific bills, lower attendance of members);**
   b) growing instances of **divisions along partisan lines** in Committee proceedings (Data Protection Bill, Waqf Bill).

3. a) Handicaps in functioning due to **absence** of **standing research support** (highlighted by NCRWC);
   b) non-consideration of **members’ own expertise** while nominating them to different committees. This, coupled with **short tenure** (1 year), leads to **ad-hocism** in their functioning.

The DRSC System is a path-breaking endeavour of **parliamentary surveillance over administration**. There is a need to improve the functioning of these **“mini-Parliaments”** through sustained **secretarial and research** **support**. This will allow them to better **guide the Executive** in the achievement of **long-term national goals**. (***404 words***)

## Q10 [Year: 2026]
**Question:** Critically discuss the need for the codification of parliamentary privileges in the country.
*Metadata: [Year: 2026] [Institute: Forum IAS] [Programme: MGP] [is_pyq: false] [Group: ] [Exam: ] [Stage: Mains] [Paper: Mains - GS 2] [Subject: POLITY] [Section Group: Organs of Government & Dispute Redressal] [Micro Topic: Parliament and State Legislatures - structure, functioning, conduct of business, powers & privileges and issues arising out of these] [Sub Topic: Parliamentary Privileges] [Macro Tag: Analytical] [Micro Tag: Critically discuss, Codification, Parliamentary privileges, India]*
#### Answer from ForumIAS

| **Approach:** • Introduce the answer by highlighting the different sources of parliamentary privileges and the absence of a definitive Code for the same. <br>• In the body, discuss the need for the codification of parliamentary privileges in the country, highlighting both the positives and negatives of the same. <br>• Conclude by underlining the need to incorporate international best practices in the exercise of privileges. |
| --- |

**Article 105 (3)** empowers **Parliament to define the Parliamentary Privileges**. However, in the **absence of** **such a law**, parliamentary privileges continue to be derived from diverse **sources**-**Constitution, statutes,** **judicial interpretation, rules of procedure** and established **conventions**.

Several experts have flagged the **ambiguity** and **the potential for their misuse** in the absence of a definitive Code of Parliamentary privileges. They have argued in **favour of such a code on account** of the following reasons:
1. The present system **equates Indian Parliament** and **British House of Commons** (despite the reference to Section 15 of 44th Amendment). While the latter is regarded as **sovereign**, the former is subject to **constitutional limitations**. A Code of Parliamentary Privileges will **uphold the supremacy** **of the Constitution** as opposed to the supremacy of Parliament.

2. a) Codification can **remove the uncertainty in their application**. This uncertainty creates **potential** **for misuse** by curbing the **freedom of speech** of the citizens (Karnataka Assembly case 2017);
   b) It can strengthen the **right of information** of the citizens (definitive guidelines to prevent arbitrary redaction of parliamentary proceedings);
   c) such a system will uphold the ideals of **constitutionalism** (limited power).

3. a) A codified framework can also provide **safeguards against attempts to stifle dissent** or suppress **minority voices** within the legislature. Protecting the **rights of individual parliamentarians** can strengthen the democratic principles of representation and pluralism;
   b) Clear guidelines and mechanisms would demonstrate the **commitment of the legislature to transparency and fairness**.

4. a) It will delineate the limits and conditions under which privileges can be invoked. This can help prevent the use of privileges as a **shield** to protect MPs from **legitimate scrutiny.**
   b) such codification will allow the **judiciary to examine the validity of privilege claims** and preserve the constitutional principle of **checks and balances**.

Despite the aforementioned advantages of codification, lawmakers have put forward the following **reasons in** **opposition to codification:**
1. a) Codification of privileges is likely to have adverse consequences for the **independence of the** **Parliament** and **separation of powers**;
   b) any law enacted for codification will be **subject to judicial** **scrutiny** for inconsistency with the **Fundamental Rights** (Patna Searchlight Case).

2. a) Any legislation aimed at codification will be a reflection of the **will of the current Executive** which holds majority. Such a law may curtail the **privileges of Opposition members**;
   b) crystallization of privileges in a code may **reduce the interpretive power of the Presiding officers.** This will impact their ability to respond to **practical challenges in the functioning** of the House.

Several **other democratic countries such as Australia** have taken concrete steps in the direction of **codification of Parliamentary privileges.** Until such time as codification takes place, **India can incorporate** **best practices** from other countries. This will help ensure that **safeguarding Parliamentary privileges** does not conflict with **rights of citizens and accountability of members**. (***453 words***)
