## Q1 [Year: 2026]
**Question:** What role do environmental NGOs and activists play in influencing Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) outcomes for major projects in India? Discuss with examples.

#### Answer from ForumIAS

| **Approach:** • Introduce the answer by defining EIA. <br>• In the body, discuss the role of environmental NGOs and activists in influencing EIA outcomes for major projects in India, with examples. <br>• Conclude by highlighting the significance of their contribution. |
| --- |

Environment Impact Assessment (EIA) can be defined as a study to predict, evaluate, and mitigate the effects of a proposed activity on the environment. As a decision-making tool, EIA compares various alternatives for a project. It seeks to identify the project configurations that minimise environmental harm while enabling economic benefits.

Environmental NGOs and activists play a significant role in influencing EIA outcomes in India, as they:
1. a) Undertake advocacy activities highlighting the significance of the EIA process (CSE’s role in shaping the EIA Notification);
   b) act as watchdogs, ensuring the integrity of the process (e.g. lapses in EIA report for Cachar airport highlighted by Forum for Social Harmony).

2. a) Aid the scientific conduct of the EIA by providing well researched inputs for the same (Centre for Science and Environment);
   b) may suggest alternative pathways for the project, which may prompt course correction enhancing the effectiveness of EIA (e.g. Bengaluru Metro Phase 3 project case).

3. a) Generate awareness among the people regarding their rights (e.g. mandatory Gram Sabha consultations under PESA in Scheduled Areas);
   b) build their capacity enabling them to give free, prior and informed consent during public hearings (e.g. actions taken by Medha Patkar and Narmada Bachao Andolan in Sardar Sarovar Dam project);
   c) act as the link between the authorities and the local population (e.g. by undertaking stakeholder mapping, submit formal comments and objections).

4. a) Bring to light glaring lapses in EIA reports, especially regarding the concerns of the affected populations (e.g. ICSF report highlighting the neglect of traditional fishing communities in the EIA report of Mumbai Coastal Road);
   b) support legal action on behalf of the people in case of violation of their rights (e.g. Goa Foundation's NGT cases against flawed EIAs).

5. a) Aid the state authorities in improving the EIA process (sharing of best practices). This helps in improving environmental governance;
   b) document and report the EIA process, thus increasing transparency in the same (e.g. role of Survival International in the Vedanta Mining case);
   c) monitor post-clearance compliance, which helps ensure accountability.

However, they can also play an obstructionist role in the (EIA) process. Critics argue that these groups can derail critical infrastructure and industrial projects by leveraging public sentiment, media, and legal systems, even when projects comply with regulatory norms. Their campaigns may rely on alarmist rhetoric, failing to consider the socio- economic benefits of such projects. Despite such criticisms, the contribution of environmental NGOs and activists to the EIA process helps ensure that economic development is firmly grounded in the principles of sustainable development and environmental justice. (390 words)

Extra marks/content differentiation:
1. History of EIA in India:A signatory to the Stockholm Declaration (1972) on Environment, India enacted laws to control water (1974) and air (1981) pollution soon after. But it was only after the Bhopal gas leak disaster in 1984 that the country legislated an umbrella Act for environmental protection in 1986. Under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, India notified its first EIA norms in 1994, setting in place a legal framework for regulating activities that access, utilise, and affect (pollute) natural resources. Every development project has been required to go through the EIA process for obtaining prior environmental clearance ever since. The 1994 EIA notification was replaced with a modified draft in 2006. In 2020, the government redrafted it again to incorporate the amendments and relevant court orders issued since 2006, and to make the EIA “process more transparent and expedient.”

2. Key provisions of Draft EIA notification 2020: ● Replacement of the incumbent 4-stage process with a 6-stage process. ● Increase in the number of project categories and change in their approval criteria. ● Comprehensive list of definitions ● Introduction of a Technical Expert Committee for categorization/re-categorization of projects ● Ex-post facto environmental clearance. ● Exemption to certain categories of projects

## Q2 [Year: 2026]
**Question:** Bring out the reasons for rising groundwater contamination in the country. Suggest remedial measures and mention the government initiatives in this regard.

#### Answer from ForumIAS

| **Approach:** • Introduce the answer by giving a brief background of rising groundwater contamination in the country through some data. <br>• In the body, first bring out the reasons for the same. <br>• Next, suggest some remedial measures. <br>• Lastly, mention the government initiatives in this regard. <br>• Conclude by emphasizing the significance of these measures and initiatives. |
| --- |

According to an assessment by the CGWB, groundwater contamination poses a severe environmental and public health challenge in India. Nearly 56% of the districts in India are affected by groundwater contamination, particularly excessive nitrate levels. Other major contaminants affecting groundwater quality are arsenic, iron, fluoride, uranium etc.

The reasons for the rising groundwater contamination in India are discussed below:
1. a) Geogenic contamination due to leaching of naturally occurring minerals into aquifers in specific geologies (e.g. Arsenic, Manganese contamination in West Bengal);
   b) coastal salinity due to entrapped sea water (connate water), sea water ingress, seepage from navigation canals and salt pans (e.g. Minjur area of Tamil Nadu));
   c) contamination enhanced by geothermal activity mobilizing naturally occurring elements into nearby aquifers (e.g. uranium and radon contamination in Himachal Pradesh).

2. a) Destruction of wetlands and mangroves which act as natural water filters;
   b) inland salinization in arid and semi-arid regions, primarily caused by rising groundwater tables (often due to irrigation seepage),  It leads to waterlogging and subsequent concentration of salts through heavy evaporation.

3. a) Excessive groundwater extraction for anthropogenic uses (domestic, industrial, agricultural). It lowers the water table, thereby increasing the concentration of contaminants (e.g. Arsenic pollution in Bihar);
   b) unscientific farming and government subsidies leading to overuse of agro-chemical inputs (fertilisers, pesticides) and subsequent groundwater contamination (nitrates and phosphates leaching - Punjab);
   c) acid mine drainage i.e. flow of acidic water from metal and coal mines;
   d) wastes from nuclear power plants, improper disposal of medical radioisotopes causing radioactive contamination.

4. a) Indiscriminate discharge of industrial-effluents;
   b) improper municipal solid waste disposal (leachates from landfills);
   c) contamination from untreated domestic sewage (only 28% of sewage generated in Indian cities is treated);
   d) overloading of sewage-treatment plants (STPs) leading to spillage of untreated water and waste (faecal coliform-contamination);
   e) contamination due to bio-medical waste (bacteria, virus, medication).

Remedial measures and government initiatives to address the problem of groundwater pollution are as follows:
1. a) ‘Crop-neutral' incentive-structures for sustainable agricultural practices;
   b) incentives to organic- farming and regulated use of agrochemicals;
   c) strict-enforcement of industrial-waste-treatment regulations;
   d) proper waste-management and landfill-design;
   e) appropriate sewage-treatment infrastructure.

2. a) Regular-monitoring and assessment of groundwater quality;
   b) public-awareness campaigns, e.g. sharing success stories of Sailam model village (Mizoram);
   c) protection of natural filters through afforestation, wetland restoration and mangrove conservation;
   d) artificial recharge and remediation (e.g. through Use of recharge pits, injection wells, aquifer flushing) to dilute the concentration of contaminants.

3. a) Groundwater-management through community-participation and capacity-building (Atal Bhujal Yojana); mapping aquifers to better understand groundwater-resources and their management (NAQUIM);
   b) real-time monitoring of industrial-effluents (OCEMS);
   c) checking river pollution (NRCP) and coastal pollution, including groundwater-contamination (ICZMP);
   d) sanitation and waste-management including reducing groundwater-pollution from domestic sources (Swachh Bharat Mission);

4. a) CGWB monitors groundwater-quality and provides guidelines for pollution-control;
   b) capacity building of local bodies for greywater management (e.g. through Finance Commission recommended grants);
   c) creation/rejuvenation of structures for water harvesting and conservation (e.g. Mission Amrit Sarovar).

5. a) Data-led water-governance (PM-GATI Shakti NMP) to enhance water-use efficiency;
   b) promote cultivation of water-efficient crops (‘Sahi Fasal Campaign’, ’National Millets Mission);
   c) water conservation practices in agriculture (AIBP, HKKP, PDMC);
   d) strategic planning and oversight of river water quality (PRAYAG, NMCG);
   e) efforts for conservation of natural ecosystems that act as water-filters (e.g. MISHTI for mangrove conservation);
   f) enhance international cooperation (Global River Cities Alliance).

These key initiatives have led to significant improvements in groundwater recharge, quality, and management (e.g. 81% of groundwater samples are suitable for irrigation- Annual Groundwater Quality Report, 2024). With a focus on sustainability and innovation, these measures ensure a secure water future for the upcoming generations.(485 words)

CGWB: Central Ground Water board; IWMI- International Water Management Institute; UNU-EHS: United Nations University - Institute for Environment and Human Security; ICZM: Integrated Coastal Zone Management; NRCP: National River Conservation Plan; NAQUIM: National Aquifer Mapping and Management Program; OCEMS: Online- Continuous-Effluent-Monitoring-System; NMP: National Master Plan; AIBP: Accelerated Irrigation Benefits Program; HKKP: Har Khet Ko Pani; PDMC: Per Drop More Crop. PRAYAG: Platform for Real-time Analysis of Yamuna, Ganga, and their tributaries; NMCG: National Mission for Clean Ganga

Environment and human health are increasingly becoming intertwined. Rising pollution, climate change, and ecosystem disruptions have altered disease patterns and reduced life expectancy. Tackling this requires an integrated institutional mechanism. India, however, lacks such a framework to deal with the overlapping issues of climate, environment, health, and the economy.

In this context, there exists a case for setting up a centralized Environment Health Regulatory Agency, which is argued below:
1. a) Increasingly visible nexus between public health and environmental degradation (e.g. respiratory disorders due to air pollution, linked to over 2mn deaths annually in India- The BMJ);
   b) emerging health challenges on account of climate change (e.g. increased prevalence of zoonotic disease as pathogens cross species barrier);
   c) exacerbation of natural disasters having profound health consequences (e.g. heat waves).

2. a) Institutional fragmentation in the domain of environmental health regulation (e.g. CPCB and SPCBs for pollution control, MoEFCC for broader environmental policy, MoHFW for disease surveillance).    b) experience from international best practices (e.g. similar bodies in USA, Germany, Japan). They show how centralized frameworks strengthen compliance, innovation, and accountability in environmental health governance.

3. a) Need to mainstream health risk evaluations in developmental projects. EHRA can make Health Impact Assessments a mandatory part of environmental clearances, aligning development with well-being;
   b) 

imperative to achieve cross-sectoral policy integration (e.g. incorporating future climate needs in health infrastructure); c) can help institutionalize the One Health approach (e.g. coordination between veterinary, agriculture, and wildlife sectors) to manage ecosystem-based health risks.

As India faces the dual challenge of ecological stress and rising health burdens, the creation of an Environmental Health Regulatory Agency can fill a critical institutional gap. It will enable India to move from reactive damage control to preventive, data-driven, and integrated environmental health governance. This will ensure sustainable development with human well-being at its core. (328 words)

Overtourism refers to situations where the number of visitors exceeds the destination’s carrying capacity. This leads to unacceptable deterioration in the quality of life and experience in the area.  Overtourism presents challenges like infrastructure stress, potential ecological degradation and alienation of local cultures. A carrying capacity-based planning approach can aid in promoting sustainable tourism in India in the following ways:
1. a) Aligns tourism with ecosystem limits, preventing irreversible ecological degradation in sensitive areas (e.g. biodiversity hotspots);
   b) helps quantify ecosystem stress thresholds, enabling science-based limits on tourist numbers in ecologically sensitive areas;
   c) facilitates incorporation of seasonality and regeneration consideration into visitor planning, allowing fragile ecosystems to recover (e.g., e-pass system in Ooty and Kodaikanal).

2. a) Identifies physical infrastructure requirements (e.g. water availability, sewage, road network) to avoid system overload in peak seasons;
   b) helps avoid reactive infrastructure expansions which may cause ecological damage  (e.g. deforestation, encroachment of blue-green infrastructure);
   c) can reduce unplanned urban sprawl around popular tourist sites (e.g., unregulated hotel growth in Mussoorie or Manali)

3. a) Aids in prioritization of local access to shared resources (water, transport, public space), causing minimum disruption to their routine lives. This reduces social conflict by ensuring tourism does not crowd out essential services for residents;
   b) maintains cultural autonomy of host communities and mitigates cultural commodification, allowing cultural practices to thrive on their own terms.

4. a) Allows a shift in focus from volume to value, enabling higher income per tourist while minimizing per capita impact;
   b) can drive resource-efficient tourism models (e.g. agritourism). These models are more suited to India’s economic realities;
   c) creates potential to integrate disaster and climate change resilience into tourism planning and development.

Carrying capacity based planning enhances the resilience of tourism, while promoting inclusivity and sustainability. It can help ensure long-term economic development, while protecting the environment and preserving social harmony. This will help uphold the vision of the National Strategy for Sustainable Tourism. ## Q5 [Year: 2026]
**Question:** What are marine heat waves? Throw light on its impact on marine ecosystem and coastal communities.
*Metadata: [Year: 2026] [Institute: Forum IAS] [Programme: MGP] [is_pyq: false] [Group: ] [Exam: ] [Stage: Mains] [Paper: Mains - GS 3] [Subject: Environment] [Section Group: Climate Change] [Micro Topic: Global warming, climate change, and international frameworks] [Sub Topic: Climate Change] [Macro Tag: Descriptive, Analytical] [Micro Tag: What are, Throw light, Marine heat waves, Ecosystem]*
#### Answer from ForumIAS

| **Approach:** • In the introduction, explain marine heat waves briefly. <br>• In the body, throw light on their impact on marine ecosystems and coastal communities. <br>• Conclude by highlighting some measures for mitigation and adaptation. |
| --- |

Marine heat waves (MHWs) are extreme weather events linked with marine areas. They occur where the surface temperature of a particular region of the sea rises by 3 to 4 ℃ above the average temperature for at least five days. Two main drivers of marine heat waves are:
1. Surface heat flux: Heating of sea surface due to presence of an atmospheric high-pressure system above a region of water for an extended period.

2. Advection: Movement of warmer waters into the region by ocean currents.

They have severe impact on marine ecosystems and coastal communities, as discussed:
1. a) Disruption of food webs due to invasive alien species, harmful algal blooms;
   b) amplify the risk of other ocean stressors like acidification, deoxygenation;
   c) can cause incidents of mass mortalities. This can happen when the threshold for thermal tolerance is breached or due to increase in hypoxic zones e.g. (“fish kills” in Gulf of Thailand and Gulf of Mexico).

2. a) Loss of ocean biodiversity due to habitat destruction (kelp forests, seagrass meadows, coral reefs are most sensitive to MHWs);
   b) forced changes in species’ behaviour (altering migration patterns). This increases the risk of harm to marine species (e.g. increase in whale and dolphin stranding in the South Pacific Ocean in January 2024).

3. a) Impact on aquaculture and fisheries (death or reduced productivity of economically important species like lobsters and snow crabs). It has a cascading effect on livelihoods and food security;
   b) harms regional tourism.

4. a) Disruption of water cycle (increased evaporation). This can cause hydrological (floods, droughts) and meteorological (wildfires, heatwaves) disasters on the land;
   b) frequent MHWs in the Indian Ocean is altering the patterns of monsoon rains (IITM study). This can severely impact agriculture and allied activities, for e.g. MHWs in Bay of Bengal in June 2023 caused extreme rainfall in usually arid north-western part of India.

5. a) Can ‘supercharge’ the heat and moisture exchanges between sea and air, which could intensify tropical cyclones (e.g. Cyclone Remal). This can have devastating consequences for coastal communities (e.g. loss of life and property)
   c) contributes to sea-level rise (due to thermal expansion of water), which can lead to coastal erosion, flooding, etc.

An analysis published in Nature Climate Change has pointed out a 240 per cent increase in the average number of marine heatwave (MHW) days in the summers of 2023-24. It is important to develop better response plans and adaptation and intervention approaches as they intensify under climate change. Measures to reduce global carbon emissions and slow down ocean warming can counteract their impact and build ocean resilience. (432 words)

## Q6 [Year: 2026]

| --- |

An urban flood is the submergence of an entire city or parts of it and the inability of the city infrastructure to deal with it. It occurs when a city receives massive levels of water due to heavy precipitation or other causes (rapid snowmelt, storm surge). Causes of urban floods in India are discussed below:
1. a) Extreme rainfall events (e.g. 155mm rainfall in a day in Delhi in July 2023). This could be due to unique meteorological factors (e.g.  combination of western disturbances and summer monsoon). They are often

beyond the coping capacity of drainage systems (e.g. Patna floods 2019); b) inundation from natural disasters (storm surge, tsunamis- e.g. flooding in Chennai due to Cyclone Fengal, 2024).

2. a) Threats posed by climate change and global warming (sea level rise, intensification of river flows due to heat, increased frequency of extreme rainfall events, increased convectivity along western coast);
   b) influence of local topography (e.g. Guwahati situated on the banks of Brahmaputra is frequently flooded) and urban heat island effect (contributes to intensification of rainfall due to localized convection) .

3. a) Unplanned urbanization causes the cities to ‘burst at their seams’ (proliferation of slums, unauthorized constructions). Such illegal construction often occupy floodplains, stormwater channels etc (e.g. Bengaluru floods 2022);
   b) decline in green and blue cover (deforestation, encroachment on floodplains);
   c) concretization of cities (impermeable footpaths, streets) and clogging of storm water drains (siltation, dumping of municipal solid waste) disrupts natural drainage;
   d) engineering deficits in urban infrastructure (recurrent flooding of Minto road area in Delhi);
   e) governance challenges (multiple agencies, inadequate capacity of urban local bodies).

In the 21st century, India’s national capital Delhi (2023) and financial capital Mumbai (2005) have faced unprecedented floods:
1. a) Caused by unusually high rainfall over a short period of time (24-48 hours) which was beyond the coping capacity of the urban infrastructure;
   b) flooding happened very quickly due to faster flow times.

2. Exacerbated by the inadequacy of the drainage system, encroachment over the city's blue and green spaces and destruction of natural ecosystems (mangroves in Mumbai, Yamuna floodplains in Delhi).

Different policies and frameworks have been evolved in India for tackling such floods:
1. a) NDMA’s urban flood guidelines 2010 underscores the different nature of urban flooding (as urbanization leads to developed catchments). It mandates master drainage plans for urban areas;
   b) National Urban Flood Risk Mitigation Project focuses on hazard mapping and preparedness.

2. a) National Disaster Management Plan (NDMP) provides comprehensive guidelines for flood management with a focus on early-warning systems, coordinated response mechanisms and integration of climate change adaptation;
   b) National Urban Policy Framework guides sustainability in urban development by integrating stormwater management and land-use planning into urbanization.

3. a) Smart Cities Mission and AMRUT entails improvement in urban infrastructure to enhance resilience; Integrated Coastal Zone Management aims to reduce flooding risk in coastal areas;
   b) ecosystem-based disaster risk reduction policies, for e.g. National Mission for Sustainable Habitat, National Water Mission, National Mission for Green India under NAPCC; INDC’s seek to cut carbon emissions and avoid climate change related risks.

The development of sponge cities and blue-green infrastructure is crucial for enhancing urban resilience against flooding, aligning with SDG 11 to make cities sustainable. (464 words)

Disaster Management (Amendment) Act 2025 seeks to mainstream disaster management in development plans. It was passed by Parliament during the Budget Session 2025. The amendment aligns with the recommendations of the 15th Finance Commission. It can strengthen the disaster management framework in the country in the following ways:
1. a) Elaborates key concepts of the disaster management cycle (e.g. new definitions for hazard, recovery etc), thereby clarifying terms crucial for disaster risk management;
   b) clarifies interpretational issues in the Act (e.g. excludes law and order situation from the ambit of man-made disasters).

2. a) Institutionalizes the paradigm of disaster risk reduction in the country’s disaster management framework (Section 2 (5) Explanation). It shifts the focus from reactive relief to proactive risk reduction;
   b) recognizes the likely impact of climate change in exacerbating disaster risks (“emerging disaster risks”), aligning with global best practices.

3. a) Establishes a comprehensive disaster database at both national and state levels. A centralized data system will enable more efficient resource allocation, better coordination, and informed decision-making during crises;
   b) strengthen the institutional apparatus by granting statutory status to the NCMC (nodal body to deal with serious disasters) and HLC (handle financial assistance to states). These changes formalize the chain of command for large disasters and funding, addressing past ad-hoc arrangements.

4. a) Vests the power to prepare national/state disaster plans in NDMA/SDMA respectively (previously restricted to approving and coordinating implementation). It is expected to produce more tailored, region- specific plans (since NDMA/SDMAs can draw on local knowledge);
   b) empowers NDMA to undertake assessment of state disaster preparedness and post-disaster responses. It also enables the NDMA to guide the preparation of disaster management plans by government departments. This effectively introduces a feedback loop to improve future planning.

5. a) Provides for periodic review (3 years) and update (5 years) of the National Disaster Management Plan by the NDMA. This promotes proactive, risk-based management;
   b) empowers state governments to constitute Urban Disaster Management Authority (UDMA) for major cities. It will help address the growing incidence of urban disasters (e.g. urban fires, stampedes);

   b) facilitates the cultivation of high value crops (aromatic herbs, flowers) through extended growing seasons. This helps raise farm incomes;
   c) reduces economic (power, irrigation) and ecological (reduced fossil fuel usage due to electrification) costs. This lowers the ecological footprint of agriculture;
   e) improves the energy efficiency of greenhouse operations;
   f) offers scope for on-farm value addition (e.g. surplus electricity can be used to develop cold storage facilities).

2. a) Enhances reliability of power supply to the agricultural sector (on-farm power generation);
   b) helps check soil erosion and land degradation while increasing groundwater retention. This allows cultivation of crops on previously uncultivable areas;

   d) promotes rural electrification, income diversification (supply of excess power to grid) and livelihood generation (e.g. maintenance technicians).

4. a) Facilitates decentralized power generation. This helps in resolving the concerns from the concentration of India’s renewable energy generation (in western and southern parts). It, thus, enhances energy supply stability;
   b) benefits accrued to discoms (limits cross subsidized agriculture power tariffs that cause financial stress;
   c) reduction in T&D losses help in the improvement of the entire power supply chain.

1. a) Agrivoltaic systems require customized mounting that raises panel height for crops. This makes them costlier than ground-mounted solar farms;
   b) likely mismatch between electricity generation and crop cycles (due to seasonal variability in sunshine availability).

2. a) Setting up agrivoltaics on fertile lands may reduce crop yield. This is especially true for food crops that require full sunlight (e.g. wheat. There is inadequate research available on Crop-Panel compatibility (i.e. shade tolerant varieties);
   b) impact on day-to-day agricultural operations (possible inconvenience of use) and issue of safety (electric wiring).

3. a) Expected low returns on investment (low solar power tariff);
   b) lack of standardized designs and installation;

   b) restrictive regulatory frameworks (e.g. need for land use conversion, grid connectivity permissions);
   c) cultural resistance to adoption due to lack of awareness and sensitization.

Agrivoltaics, that amounts to harvesting the sun twice, offers a sustainable path for India's agricultural and energy sectors. According to UNDRR, disaster resilience means the ability of communities to anticipate, accommodate, and recover from disasters while maintaining essential functions. It is determined by the degree to which the social system is capable of enhancing its capacity to learn from past disasters. This leads to better future protection and improved risk reduction measures.

A disaster resilient framework consists of diverse elements as discussed:
1. a) Hazard identification, exposure mapping and vulnerability assessment across social, economic and environmental dimensions;
   b) planning for the disaster through mitigation (earthquake proofing of building codes) and adaptation measures (drought resilient crop varieties).

2. a) Infrastructure evaluation, especially critical infrastructure (health, communication);
   b) development of infrastructural (cyclone shelters) and institutional capacity (National Disaster Management Plan);
   c) capacity building of the community (awareness generation, training, knowledge creation through NIDM)

3. a) Augmentation of the resource base of the economy (financial, technological) to deal with disaster comprehensively;
   b) ensuring availability of human capital (trained personnel, social networks, social cohesion and trust, local leadership);
   c) strengthening social security architecture (e.g. risk coverage through insurance).

4. a) Mounting an effective response to the disaster through a whole of society (civil society organizations, volunteer groups) and a whole of government approach (multi-agency coordination);
   b) effective rehabilitation and recovery from the disaster (Build Back Better approach).

The Sendai Framework is the successor of the Hyogo Framework for Action and outlines 7 global targets to be achieved by 2030: I. Substantially reduce: (a) Global disaster mortality (lower average per 100,000 global mortality). (b)The number of affected people (lower average global figure per 100,000). (c) Direct disaster economic loss in relation to global GDP. (d) Damage to critical infrastructure and disruption of basic services (health and education)

II. Substantially increase: (e) Number of countries with national and local DRR strategies by 2020. (f) International cooperation to developing countries to complement their national actions. (g) Availability of and access to multi-hazard early warning systems and disaster risk information and assessments.

The Disaster Management (Amendment) Act, 2025 marks a significant step towards mainstreaming disaster resilience in development planning. It is expected to go a long way in building a disaster-resilient Bharat. (326 words)
