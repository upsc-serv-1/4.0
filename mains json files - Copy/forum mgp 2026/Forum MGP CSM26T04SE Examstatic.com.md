| --- |

**Potential GDP** is defined as the **level of output** an economy can **sustain at full capacity utilization** and **full** **employment** without creating **inflationary pressures**. It represents the optimal use of resources (capital, labour, etc.) at sustainable levels. Following are the factors that **prevent India** from realizing its **potential GDP:**
1. a) In the agriculture sector, **fragmented landholdings** (average 1.08 ha in 2015-16) hinder **mechanization** and limit **production scale**;
   b) inadequate **storage and processing** infrastructure reduces **value addition**;
   c) poor **price discovery** due to **market fragmentation** collectively restrict agriculture's GDP contribution to 16% (ES 24-25), despite employing a large workforce (46.1% - ES 24- 25).

2. a) In the industrial sector, low **technology adoption** remains a significant challenge;
   b) the **'missing** **middle'** phenomenon and regulations that **discourage business growth** (dwarfism) create a **low** **productivity trap**;
   c) these factors reduce **competitiveness** and prevent integration into **global value** **chains**, leading to a **stagnant GDP contribution** (~ 30%) for decades.

3. a) **Global economic slowdown** and **increasing protectionism** limit India's export potential and foreign investment inflows;
   b) significant **skills gap** (only 2.3% of workers formally skilled) restricts **productivity growth**.

4. a) Declining **Gross Fixed Capital Formation (GFCF)** from 40% (2011) to 33.5% (2024) indicates inadequate **infrastructure investment**;
   b) **elevated logistics costs** (14% of GDP) reduce **competitiveness** of Indian goods in global markets.

Measures that can be taken **to realize potential GDP:**
1. a) **Increasing agricultural productivity** through farmer collectives (FPOs) to improve **bargaining** **power** and achieve **economies of scale**;
   b) promoting **land consolidation methods** (cooperative and corporate farming) to enable mechanization;
   c) implementing **digital market platforms** (e-NAM) for transparent **price discovery**.

2. a) Developing **integrated value chains** (PM Formalization of Food Processing Enterprises) to reduce post-harvest losses; enhancing **export competitiveness** of agricultural products through **dedicated** **infrastructure** [Trade Infrastructure for Export Scheme (TIES)] and market development initiatives.

3. a) **Fostering entrepreneurship** to generate quality employment (Startup India Seed Fund, MUDRA loans);
   b) increasing **women's economic participation** to utilize the untapped labor resource;
   c) addressing the **skills mismatch** through targeted vocational training programs (Skill India Mission).

4. a) Stimulating **indigenous innovation** (Atal Innovation Mission) to reduce technology dependence;
   b) **investing strategically** in high-growth sectors (through the Rs. 1.5 lakh crore 50-year interest-free loan, Budget 2025-26).

5. a) Accelerating **technological modernization** across manufacturing (PLI schemes);
   b) enhancing **global market integration** through strategic trade agreements (India-EFTA TEPA) to position Indian industries within global **value chains**.

Bridging India's potential-actual **GDP gap** requires coordinated **short and long-term policies** that strengthen institutions and remove **structural barriers** to achieve sustainable economic transformation. (***413 words***)

## Q3 [Year: 2026]
**Question:** To what extent, in your opinion, is GDP the best measure of a country's development? 10
*Metadata: [Year: 2026] [Institute: Forum IAS] [Programme: MGP] [is_pyq: false] [Group: ] [Exam: ] [Stage: Mains] [Paper: GS1] [Subject: INDIAN ECONOMY] [Section Group: Macroeconomics & Fiscal Policy] [Micro Topic: Indian Economy and issues relating to planning, mobilization of resources, GDP, and monetary framework] [Sub Topic: GDP] [Macro Tag: Analytical] [Micro Tag: To what extent, GDP, Development]*
#### Answer from ForumIAS

| **Approach:** • Introduce the answer by defining GDP. <br>• In the body, first discuss why GDP is considered as the best measure of a country's development. <br>• Then, present the counter-arguments. <br>• Conclude by highlighting the need to supplement GDP with other metrics. |
| --- |

GDP (Gross Domestic Product) represents the **aggregate monetary value** of **final goods and services** produced within a country during a specific **time period**. It can be calculated through three methods viz. **Income, Expenditure, and Product** or **Value-added.** GDP is considered the **best measure** of a country's development due to following **reasons**:
1. a) **Broad consensus** among economists and policymakers;
   b) provides **standardized data** enabling cross-country and temporal **comparisons** (e.g., comparing economic performance between India and China over decades).

2. a) Can be **disaggregated** by sectors, regions, and districts;
   b) allows both **macroeconomic** and **microeconomic analysis** (e.g., identifying which economic sectors are driving growth).

3. a) Relies on **quantifiable economic outputs** rather than **subjective assessments** (e.g., happiness indices);
   b) provides critical **economic signals** to various **stakeholders**;
   c) guides **policy decisions** and **investment strategies** (e.g., interest rates decisions by Central Banks).

However, critics argue that GDP has **significant limitations** as a development measure, such as:
1. a) Measures **economic output** but not **overall well-being** or quality of life;
   b) fails to capture **development dimensions** like health, education, and environmental quality, for e.g. India's consistent poor ranking in indices like  World Happiness Report, Global Gender Gap Report etc.
   c) does not reflect **income distribution** or **regional disparities** (e.g., South Africa's high GDP per capita masks extreme **inequality**).

2. a) Counts only **monetized transactions** and  disregards valuable **unpaid contributions** to society (domestic labour, caregiving);
   b) **does not capture the externalities** (positive or negative) leading to **overestimation or underestimation of welfare**. E.g., an oil refinery polluting a nearby river; ease of living due to construction of roads.

While GDP remains a valuable economic indicator, a **comprehensive development assessment** requires **supplementary metrics** like HDI, MPI, and SDG indicators. These measures provide a more holistic understanding of **social progress**, **environmental sustainability**, and overall well-being beyond economic production alone. ***(303 words)***

**Female Labour Force Participation Rate (FLFPR)** is the **ratio** of the number of women who are **part of the** **labour force** to the number of women in the **working age**. According to **PLFS**, the FLFPR has increased from **23.3% (2017-18) to 41.7% (2023-2024)**.

**Fig. Female Labour Force Participation Rate** (Usual status, 15 years and above)

Following **factors** has contributed to the rise in FLFPR in India:
1. a) **Economic necessity** of more earning hands (nuclear families, inflation)
   b) changing **social norms** due to **modernization** (greater acceptance of working women, shifting gender roles, women role- models, etc.).

2. a) **Rising casual wages** driving **family businesses** to **utilize household female labour** over external hiring (share of helpers in household enterprises grew from 38.7% 2017-18 to 42.3% in 2023-24);
   b) 

improvements in **women’s access to education**, particularly higher education (GER for women at 28.5% vs 28.3% for men).

3. a) **Feminization of agriculture** (57% in 2017-18 to 64.4% in 2023-24) driven by **male migration** to non- agricultural sectors;
   b) growth in **self-employment** post COVID-19 (highlighted by the Economic Survey 2024-25).

4. a) Targeted **government initiatives** aimed at **skilling** (SANKALP) and easing **credit access** (Mudra Yojana, Stand-Up India);
   b) strengthened statutory framework ensuring **motherhood support** (Maternity Benefits Amendment Act 2017) and **workplace safety** (POSH Act 2013).

5. a) Enhanced support for **women grassroots organizations** facilitating women **entrepreneurship** (WSHGs, Namo Drone Didi, Lakhpati Didi scheme);
   b) improvements in **employment estimation** methodology (better capture of female workers in unpaid work as highlighted by ES 2024-25).

6. a) Structural economic shifts and emergence of **new employment models** (Gig worker, Work-from- home) with significant **opportunities** for women (personal care services);
   b) these models enable women to **balance work** and **household responsibilities** more effectively.

This rise creates **opportunities** for inclusive development, as discussed:
1. a) Reduction in household **poverty levels** and **income inequality** in the economy;
   b) greater workforce participation by women can **reduce gender wage disparity** and enhance **gender equality.**

2. a) Could boost **economic growth** significantly (1.5% rise in GDP if FLFPR reaches 50%: World Bank);
   b) potential **spillover effects** on **education** and **healthcare** of families (women tend to invest their incomes more in these sectors: World Bank study).

3. a) Enhanced **women’s agency** (greater say in family decision making), leading to their **empowerment**;
   b) potential **shift in societal perceptions** about gender roles, promoting **social equality**.

Despite these gains, **challenges persist**, such as the concentration of women in low-paying informal jobs, workplace discrimination, ‘dual burden’, etc. Addressing these requires **gender-sensitive policies**, improved skilling, and increased formal sector opportunities. A **holistic approach** can ensure sustained FLFPR growth, fostering both economic prosperity and **gender-inclusive development** **(SDG 5)**. (***422 words***)

**Question:** What do you understand by fiscal deficit? Why is it necessary for a country to keep its fiscal deficit  under check?

| **Approach:** • Introduce by defining fiscal deficit. <br>• In the body, discuss the reasons that make it crucial for a country to control its fiscal deficit. <br>• Conclude with suggestions to improve fiscal management in the country. |
| --- |

Fiscal deficit refers to the difference between the **government's total expenditure** and its **total receipts** excluding borrowing. It indicates the **total borrowings** needed by the government to **finance its spending** beyond available revenue.

**Controlling fiscal deficit is crucial** for a country due to following **reasons**:
1. a) High fiscal deficits can trigger **macroeconomic instability** (inflation, currency depreciation, and high interest rates) e.g., Sri Lanka's recent economic crisis resulting from unsustainable deficits;
   b) erode **investor confidence**;
   c) damage a country's **creditworthiness (credit rating downgrades)** making it difficult for the government to borrow from international markets.

2. a) Persistent deficits accumulate as **public debt** and raise the risk of **debt traps** if borrowing becomes unsustainable;
   b) create **structural impediments** to effective **monetary policy implementation** (Central banks are forced to monetize debt)

3. a) Excessive government borrowing can **crowd out private investment** and raise economy-wide **borrowing costs** (due to increased demand for credit);
   b) **discourage** both domestic and foreign **investment** in productive sectors (high capital cost);
   c) reduce **capital formation** necessary for economic growth.

4. a) High fiscal deficits and accrued debt place a **disproportionate burden** on future generations, impacting **intergenerational equity**;
   b) necessitates fiscal adjustments (**higher taxes** or **reduced** **public services**), burdening common citizens.

5. a) Elevated deficits reduce **fiscal space** for implementing **counter-cyclical measures**;
   b) limits government's ability to respond effectively during **economic downturns or emergencies** (e.g., Global recession, Pandemic).

Maintaining **fiscal discipline** through **FRBM targets** is essential for macroeconomic stability. The **N.K. Singh** **Committee's** recommendations for the establishment of a **Fiscal Council**, **debt-based rules**, and **escape** **clauses** provide a pragmatic framework to balance **fiscal prudence** with **growth imperatives**. ***( 266 words)***

Fiscal policy refers to the government's use of **expenditure and revenue measures** to influence various aspects of the national economy- levels of **economic activity, employment, price stability,** etc. In a developing country like India, fiscal policy holds **immense significance**, especially in **ensuring** **sustainable development,** as discussed:
1. Enhancing people’s **disposable incomes** and **standard of living** through **wealth redistribution** and reducing **income inequality. (SDG 10)**
   a. **Income support** schemes (PM-KISAN) and provision of b**asic goods and services** through dedicated schemes (PMAY, NFSA).

   b. a) Reduction in **personal income taxes**;
   b) **progressive taxation** policies (higher proportion of direct tax) and generational **wealth redistribution** (wealth tax, inheritance tax);
   c) access to **basic** **goods and services** (lower GST on items of mass consumption).

2. Provides fiscal stimulation and enhances **economic competitiveness** through infrastructural and industrial development (**SDG 9**).
   a. 
   a) Capital expenditure** (11,11,111cr for 2024-25) creates a crowd**-in effec**t for private investment;**
   b) support for the **manufacturing sector** (PLIs) and enhancing **job creation** (ELIs)
   c) **strategic disinvestment policy** to optimize **resource allocation** (Air India, BPCL disinvestment) and improve **efficiency**.

   b. 
   a) Tax incentives** for **infrastructure development** (Section 80IA deductions, infrastructure bond benefits);**
   b) **targeted tax measures** to boost industrial growth (corporate tax cuts) and **entrepreneurship** (abolition of angel tax);
   c) **infrastructure-specific levies** (infrastructure cess) to fund development projects.

3. Provision for **social support system. (SDG 3,4,6):**
   a. a) Expenditure on **public health** infrastructure, **education** infrastructure (PM-Shri Schools) leading to better health (reduced MMR) and education outcomes (near 100% GER in primary education);
   b) **social security** through dedicated schemes (NSAP, One Rank One Pension).

   b. a) Building **health and education services** (health and education cess);
   b) allocation from the **Universal Service Obligation Fund** to improve **digital infrastructure** and connectivity in underserved areas.

4. Furthering **inclusive growth** and **human development**. (**SDG 1,2,6**)
   a. a) **Gender equity** through gender-responsive budgeting and supportive institutions (One Stop Centres)
   b) empowerment of **vulnerable sections** of society (PM JANMAN)
   c) **poverty alleviation** measures (MGNREGA) and schemes for **nutritional security** (PM POSHAN).

   b. 
   a) Tax incentives for women** (lower stamp duty on women owned properties);**
   b) agricultural promotion by **exempting agricultural income** from tax.

5. Promote **desirable consumption** patterns to achieve **sustainable growth**. (**SDG 7,11,12**)
   a. a) Access to **clean energy** sources (PM Ujjwala Yojana; PM Surya Ghar Bijli), reducing **carbon** **footprint** (FAME scheme).

   b. a) **Sin tax** on energy generation from fossil fuels (clean energy cess on coal);
   b) **excise and** **customs relief** to increase availability of components for renewable energy projects (solar panels);
   c) **green financing instruments** (Green Bonds).

India's developmental journey (**Viksit Bharat 2047**) requires balancing **growth with equity**. Within a framework of **prudent deficit management** (FRBM Act), and complemented by appropriate **monetary policy**, fiscal measures create an **enabling environment** for **inclusive** and **sustainable development**.
