**Question:** What is delimitation, and why is it important? What challenges are associated with the delimitation  exercise in India?
*Metadata: [Year: 2026] [Institute: Forum] [Programme: MGP] [is_pyq: false] [Group: UPSC CSE] [Exam: Mains] [Stage: Mains] [Paper: Mains - GS 1]*
#### Answer from ForumIAS

| **Approach:** • Introduce the answer by defining delimitation. <br>• In the body, first highlight its importance. <br>• Next, discuss challenges associated with the delimitation exercise in India. <br>• Conclude by underlining the need for consensus in the impending delimitation exercise. |
| --- |

**Delimitation** means the process of **fixing limits or boundaries** of **territorial constituencies** of a legislative body such as Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assembly. In **India**, the task of delimitation is assigned to a **Delimitation Commission**, a statutory body that has been **periodically constituted (1952, 1963, 1973 and** **2002).**

Significance of the delimitation exercise can be gauged from the fact that it is **prescribed in the Constitution** itself (**Article 82**). It is **important** due to following reasons:
1. a) Re-emphasizes the **principle of political equality**, i.e., “one person, one vote, one value.”;
   b) corrects **“malapportionment”** in Parliament/Assemblies, ensuring **equitable protection** of interests of states/regions.

2. a) Enables **increase in the number of elected representatives** for **effective representation** (rise in the average population per seat);
   b) corrects **distortion in size of constituencies** by accounting for **other demographic changes** (intra-state and inter-state migration, urbanization).

3. Examines need to **increase** **representation for marginalized communities** such as SCs and STs (Article 330,332). This is necessary to **adjust to population growth rate** among them and on account of **more communities getting added to the list** (Article 341,342).

4. Provisions of **106th Constitutional Amendment (women’s reservation** in Parliament/State Legislatures) can be enforced only after **post-Census delimitation**.

However, the following **challenges** are associated with the delimitation exercise:
1. a) States with **effective family planning programs** will see **decreased representation** (E.g. southern states);
   b) may **fuel regionalism**, creating a **north-south divide**. E.g. TN assembly resolution against delimitation.

2. a) **Smaller states** (Punjab, Goa) may get disadvantaged compared to **larger states** (Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Maharashtra);
   b) delimitation process in **sensitive areas** may aggravate **security risks** (north- eastern states).

3. a) May marginalize **Prime Ministerial aspirations** from **smaller states**;
   b) reinforce the **high political** **significance** of “**Hindi heartland**” in national politics;
   b) greater increase in seats from **urban areas** compared to **rural areas** (expected increase in urban population to 40% by 2036- World Bank).

4. a) Delimitation **changes political equations** by altering **demographic concentration** and l**ocal** **power dynamics** (Assam delimitation 2023); re-organization of constituencies may **not align with** **existing boundaries** creating **administrative difficulties (**J&K delimitation exercise 2022).

As per **Article 82,** the **next delimitation exercise** is supposed to be undertaken **after 2026.** In a democratic set up, it is important that the **upcoming exercise** is carried out **with consensus** to realize the vision of the founding fathers in the **Amrit Kaal.** (***375 words***)

| --- |

The **Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI**) is the **regulatory body** that oversees the f**unctioning of** **the capital market**, protects the **interests of investors** and ensures the **market operates in a fair and** **transparent manne**r. It derives its **powers primarily from the SEBI Act, 1992**. To fulfill its legislative mandate, the following **steps** have been taken by the SEBI:
1. a) Introduced reforms to **strengthen the regulatory framework**. E.g. insider trading regulations, implementation of the electronic trading system;
   b) brought in **ease of access** (pioneering T+0 settlement).

2. a) Ensured **ease of** **compliance** for market participants (exempting FPIs from additional disclosure requirements);
   b) developed dedicated **grievance redressal mechanism** (SCORES);
   c) has taken **action against rating agencies** (E.g. India Ratings & Research) for failure to adhere to circular requirements.

3. a) Instituted i**nvestor protection measures** (time stamping of contracts, investor protection fund, regulation of algo trading);
   b) has undertaken **innovative market mechanisms** (Social stock exchange);
   c) integrated **sustainability into business operations** (Business Responsibility & Sustainability Reporting).

4. a) Displayed proactiveness in **adapting to emerging trends** (constituted the Committee on Financial and Regulatory Technologies);
   b) taken steps to **align** with **global standards** (IOSCO principles);
   c) collaborated with international peers for **cross-border investigations (**bi-lateral MoUs with securities regulators of 29 countries).

5. a) Enabled improvements in market **efficiency and transparency** (disclosure frameworks, listing norms);
   b) has introduced **computerization** of stock exchanges **reducing transaction costs**.

Despite these credible achievements, the following **shortcomings** have been observed in SEBI’s functioning:
1. a) Challenges in timely detection and prevention of **insider trading** (ABC Bearings case, 2023);
   b) gaps in the **regulation of market intermediaries** (brokers, portfolio managers);
   c) **lack of timely action** against alleged violation of regulations (IL&FS crisis, stock price manipulation by Adani group).

2. a) Deficiencies in **investor protection framework** resulting in significant **losses for retail investors** (short-selling after Hindenburg report, F&O trading);
   b) inability to curb the **proliferation of** **"finfluencers"** spreading misinformation;
   c) issues related to **IPO overpricing.**

3. a) Limited **consultation with market participants** resulting in **restrictive regulations** (delayed operationalization of REITs);
   b) allegation of **conflict of interest** against key SEBI officials eroding **public trust** in the body;
   c) **limited penetration of capital market** (only 5% of Indians invest in markets).

**SEBI's efforts** in regulating India's capital market **are notable, but challenges persist.** **Strengthening** **regulatory framework, enforcement capability** and a more **proactive approach** in detecting and preventing market manipulation are imperative for enhancing its efficacy. *(****392 words)***

| --- |

National Human Rights Commission (NHRC) is a **statutory** body established under the **Protection of Human** **Rights Act, 1993.** In conformity with the **Paris Principles,** NHRC is an embodiment of **India’s concern for** **promotion and protection of human rights**. However, the following **structural and practical limitations** hinder the effective performance of its role:
1. a) Considered a “**toothless tiger**” on account of the **non-binding** nature of its recommendations;
   b) lack of **enforcement capabilities** and inability to **punish violators;**
   c) limited to investigating cases within **one year of occurrence** (unless exceptional circumstances justify relaxation).

2. a) **Limited jurisdiction** over human rights violations by **armed forces** ( can only seek a report, only make recommendations**) and** private individuals or entities**;**
   b) concerns about **independence from** **the government** (membership, appointment procedure);
   c) lack of **sufficient diversity** among members of the Commission (women, tribals, minorities).

3. a) Insufficient **resource base** (manpower, financial), coupled with an **overwhelming workload**, hinder its ability to fulfill its mandate;
   b) **low public awareness** about the human rights regime in India translates to **limited public engagement** with the NHRC.

These challenges underline the need to implement the following **reforms** to improve its functioning:
1. a) Strengthening NHRC's authority by **mandating government compliance** with its recommendations;
   b) ensuring **flexibility in accepting older cases** where justified (recommended by A.M. Ahmadi Committee);
   c) imposing reasonable **time limits** to enhance the credibility of investigations.

2. a) **Expanding investigative authority** to include cases involving para-military forces, private entities or individuals;
   b) **diversifying the membership** through greater involvement of civil society members, human rights activists, and experts;
   c) **capacity building** of members/staff for handling complex cases of human rights’ violations.

3. a) Equipping the Commission with necessary **resource base** (manpower, finances);
   b) guaranteeing its **financial independence** from the Government (reducing reliance on Central government grants under Section 32).

4. a) Propagation of **human rights education** among the masses to develop a **culture of respect for** **human rights**;
   b) adherence to **international standards** (GANHRI) which will help raise institutional credibility in the public mind.

Implementing these reforms will better equip the Commission to perform its role as a **guardian** **of human rights** in India. A stronger NHRC will be able to uphold its motto “**Sarve Bhavantu Sukhinah”**  and ensure a **life of** **dignity** to every Indian. ***(396 words)***

***GANHRI:** Global Alliance of National Human Rights Institutions.

The Model Code of Conduct (MCC) is a **set of guidelines** published by the Election Commission of India **(ECI)** for political parties and candidates to set **standards of conduct** during the election campaign and polling. However, the **effectiveness** of these guidelines is being increasingly **called into question** due to **recurrent** **violations** (>1000 violations during Delhi elections 2025), including by **high constitutional authorities** (Azam Khan case).

As a result, some experts have suggested granting **MCC statutory status** to strengthen the **ECI’s enforcement** **powers** to conduct free and fair elections in the following ways:
1. a) ECI will have **greater authority** to **penalize violations**;
   b) **legal consequences** rather than mere reprimands will **deter** **non-compliance.**

2. Use of **ECI’s plenary power** to enforce the MCC may **possibly encroach** on **Parliament’s** **jurisdiction**. A law is likely to avoid this through **definitive provisions**.

3. It can resolve the absence of **immediate grievance redressal** mechanisms for any concerns of stakeholders in the election process (cancellation of nomination). **Presently,** the decision can only be challenged in the **High Court** after the announcements of results.

4. It would ensure a **uniform application** of the MCC, **eliminating inconsistencies** in its enforcement.

However, other stakeholders, including the ECI have **opposed providing statutory backing** to the MCC for the following reasons:
1. a) Elections are completed in a relatively **short time** and **judicial proceedings** are longer;
   b) a law will replace the **summary procedure** for **prompt action** with a lengthy judicial procedure.
   c) judicial interventions may **complicate the managemen**t of the election process by exposing it to **excessive** **litigation**.

2. a) Statutorization would entail **enhancing administrative capacity** of ECI to handle the **increased** **responsibilities** and **legal processes;**
   b) political parties may **resist** statutory backing, perceiving it as a **constraint** on their campaign activities.

3. a) Many provisions of the MCC are **already enforceable** through other legislations like RPA and IPC (violation of secrecy of voting, causing enmity among communities).
   b) The broad objectives of MCC are best achieved by **voluntary compliance** and **oversight of an impartial watchdog. Violations** incur **reprimands** from the EC, causing significant **embarrassment** for political actors (media scrutiny, societal backlash).

Though MCC has no statutory backing it carries significant **moral weight.** Given the **practical difficulties in** **giving statutory backing** to the MCC, another alternative would be **amending RPA** acts to deal with specific issues of electoral malpractices. (***387 words***)

## Q7 [Year: 2026]
**Question:** What steps are required for the constitutionalization of a Commission? Do you think granting  constitutional status to the Central Information Commission (CIC) would strengthen the RTI regime in  India? Give reasons.
*Metadata: [Year: 2026] [Institute: Forum] [Programme: MGP] [is_pyq: false] [Group: UPSC CSE] [Exam: Mains] [Stage: Mains] [Paper: Mains - GS 1]*
#### Answer from ForumIAS

| **Approach:** • Give an introduction explaining the term constitutionalization. <br>• In the body, first list the steps needed for constitutionalization of a commission. <br>• Next, discuss the ways in which constitutionalization of the Central Information Commission could strengthen the RTI regime. <br>• Lastly, bring out the challenges of constitutionalization. <br>• Conclude by highlighting the need to take other measures to strengthen the RTI regime. |
| --- |

**Constitutionalization** of a body refers to the process of **incorporating provisions** related to its composition, functions, and powers directly into a **country's Constitution**, thereby elevating its **legal status** and **institutional significance**.

The process would require an **amendmen**t to the Constitution under **Article 368** and can be done in following **steps**:
1. Introduction of a **Constitution Amendment Bill** in either House detailing the **commission’s** **composition, mandate, powers,** jurisdiction, etc.

2. **Scrutinising and passage** of the bill by **both the Houses** through **special majority** (majority of the total membership and 2/3rd members present and voting).

3. **Ratification** by half of the **State Legislatures** (if affecting the federal provisions) E.g. NJAC
4. **Presidential assent** to the amendment (and consequent publication in the **official** **gazette)** would result in **constitutionalisation** of the commission.

5. For example, in 2018, the National Commission for Backward Classes **(NCBC)** was given **constitutional status** (338B) through the **102nd Amendment Act**.

The Central Information Commission (**CIC**) is currently a **statutory body** established under the **Right to** **Information** Act, 2005. Granting it **constitutional status** could **strengthen the RTI regime** in the following ways:
1. Raise its **institutional standing** and **elevate** the CIC's position in the **governance hierarchy**, giving its orders greater **legal weight, strengthen enforcement** of its decisions and improve **compliance by** **public authorities**.

2. Would safeguard the CIC from efforts of **transient legislative majorities** to curb its autonomy (RTI Amendment Act 2019). This will promote l**ong-term continuity of the RTI regime** itself.

3. Constitutional recognition would enhance the **institutional legitimacy of the CIC**, reinforcing **public** **trust** in the RTI framework. This would signal a strong commitment to **transparency, accountability,** and **citizen empowerment** in governance.

4. Granting constitutional status to the CIC can be considered the **next logical step** after the **Right to** **Information** has been judicially recognized as part of **Article 19 and 21**.

5. It may also enable **the CIC with financial and administrative autonomy,** helping address issues such as prolonged **vacancies and funding constraints** and empower it to **address regional disparities** in the implementation of the Act.

Constitutionalization, however, is **unlikely to address** the following challenges of the RTI regime:
1. Limited **implementation capacity** in government departments due to **shortage of dedicated** **personnel** (officials often performing **dual roles** as PIOs) and funds.

2. Repeated resort to **Section 8 (1)** of the RTI Act to deny information to citizens; restrictive laws (Official Secrets Act) that promote a general **culture of secrecy** in government functioning.

3. Weaker **whistle-blower protection mechanisms** and challenges of **safety and security of RTI** **activists**  (>100 RTI activists killed since 2005).

4. Constitutionalization may introduce an element of **rigidity in the RTI** regime and create potential for **institutional conflict** due to **overlapping jurisdictions.**

While granting constitutional status to the **CIC could strengthen its authority**, it is **not a panacea** for all challenges. **Complementary reforms** (increasing capacity of government departments, suo-moto disclosures, operationalising whistleblower protection mechanisms) need to be implemented to **strengthen the RTI regime**. ***(408 words)***
