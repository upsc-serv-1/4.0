| **Approach:** • Introduce the answer by explaining the ‘Missing Middle’ problem in Indian manufacturing. <br>• In the body, elaborate on the factors responsible for the same. <br>• Conclude by suggesting corrective measures. |
| --- |

**‘Missing Middle’** refers to the phenomenon of a relatively **lower share of mid-sized firms** in the Indian manufacturing sector in comparison to the shares of **small-sized** and the **large-sized firms**. It reflects an ‘**economic paradox’** where mid-sized firms face large firms’ regulatory burdens without their scale benefits while losing small enterprises’ policy advantages and flexibility. The problem of ‘Missing Middle’ is attributed to **multiple factors**:
1. a) **Policy environment** encouraging smaller firms and discouraging larger ones (IDR Act 1951, Industrial Policy 1956, MRTP Act);
   b) **market conditions** favouring large sized firms to achieve **economies of scale**;
   c) varied challenges in **ease of doing business** (contract enforcement, municipal permissions, **higher compliance burden**).

2. a) Labour market regulations** discourage middle level firms (mandatory PF in firms hiring over 20 workers);**
   b) **under-reporting** of employment to **evade regulations** (mandatory registration under Factories Act if the number of workers exceed 10);
   c) high **entry** and **exit barriers** for large firms (rigid hire and fire policies).

3. a) Lack of **transitionary support** from small to mid-sized firms;
   b) **infrastructure constraints** (energy, transportation, storage) restrict growth potential;
   c) limited access to **skilled workers** and high dependence on **informal labour**.

4. a) Inability to maintain **product quality standards** due to scale-related constraints and lack of advanced technology;
   b) **capital constraints** (high interest rates) hinder investment in business expansion;
   c) inadequate resources to maintain **sustainability** and adopt **eco-friendly practices**;
   d) competition from **cheap imports** (China, Bangladesh, Vietnam).

Along with the **‘Make in India’** initiative, **India also needs** to nurture the small and micro firms to grow into mid or large-sized firms. This can be achieved through **labour law reforms, technological upgradation** and supply chain development, among other measures. The upcoming **“grand necklace of Industrial Smart Cities”** is expected to contribute significantly to addressing the issue. *(****297 words****)*

**IDR Act:** Industries (Development and Regulation) Act, 1951**; MRTP Act:** Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act

**Global Capability Centers (GCCs)**, also called Global In-house Centers (GICs), are **offshore** facilities set up by **multinational corporations** (MNCs) to manage a variety of **business functions and processes** for their **parent organizations**. According to an ANSR report, India has **more than 50 %** of the Global GCCs. They hold immense **significance** for the country’s economic landscape, as discussed:
1. a) Create **high quality employment** opportunities (product managers, analysts), with nearly 1.9 million direct employment (ES 2024-25);
   b) facilitate **skill development** through exposure to global best practices;
   c) enhance **economic competitiveness** driven by **tech transfers** through R&D projects.

2. a) Catalyze growth in **high-end engineering R&D** (aerospace, defence, semiconductors), expanding 1.3 times faster than overall GCC setup (ES 2024-25);
   b) accelerate **leadership development** among India’s middle-management talent, with **global leadership roles** in GCCs expected to **grow** **exponentially** (from 6500 to 30000 by 2030: ES 2024-25).

3. a) Gives fillip to the phenomenon of **reverse brain-drain**;
   b) key role in establishing India's reputation as a **knowledge economy destination;**
   c) boost India’s **export earnings** (reduced CAD) and GDP growth (service exports constitute ~10% of India’s GDP).

4. a) Increase **FDI inflow for setup and expansion** (services, computer software and hardware sectors being top FDI recipients);
   b) boost **ancillary industries** (transportation, hospitality, real estate) due to **spillover effect**.

However, there are also **challenges** and **risks** associated with GCCs:
1. a) Skewed **wage inflation** in certain sectors (driven by rising demand and high purchasing power of MNCs);
   b) **changing dynamics** between MNCs and domestic companies (from customer to competitors);
   c) potential erosion of **local work culture** and increased influence of **western practices** (e.g. Halloween holidays).

2. a) Asymmetric fight for resources resulting in **unethical business practices** (e.g. poaching employees);
   b) exposed the Indian economy to **vulnerabilities** created by **global economic shifts** (e.g. imported inflation).

3. a) Possible disparities in **inter-regional development** (e.g. In Q3 2024, Bengaluru accounted for 62% of office leasing by GCCs);
   b) concerns regarding **data security** and **privacy.** This also involves issues about **data sovereignty** related to **cross-border data transfers** (due to blurred boundaries between parent MNC and Indian GIC).

To address these challenges and cement India’s position as the **“GCC Capital of the World”**, an overarching policy framework for **streamlining** and **simplifying regulatory processes** is needed. **Maharashtra’s IT/ITES** **Policy 2023** is a step in the right direction that can be improved and replicated nationwide. *(****394 words****)*

The "**Gig Economy**" refers to a labour market characterized by **short-term**, **freelance**, and **on-demand work** **arrangements,** rather than traditional full-time employees. It is often **facilitated by digital platforms** (e.g.,

Ola/Uber drivers, food delivery partners, web developers, etc.) According to the **NITI Aayog’s report** titled 'India's Booming Gig and Platform Economy', India's gig workforce is expected to expand to **2.35 crore by 2029-**
**30.**

**Positive implications** of its rise on the employment scenario in India:
1. a) Offers **flexibility to workers**, allowing them to choose **when, where,** and **how much** they work;
   b) benefits individuals who prefer **adjustable** **work schedules** (students, caregivers);
   c) facilitates increased **participation of women** in the workforce through **specialized roles (**personal care segment), allows them to better **balance work-life** responsibilities (cited as one of the reasons for the growth in FLFPR).

2. a) Provides an opportunity for people to **diversify their income** and supplement their primary earnings;
   b) allows acquisition of **new skills** and **valuable experience**, which can enhance **employability** in the long run;
   c) helps in bridging **regional gaps** and overcoming **geographical barriers** (flexibility to work from different locations).

3. a) Holds the potential to create **new jobs for diverse skill sets** (drivers, carpenters) thereby enabling the individuals to monetize them;
   b) enables **gradual formalization** of traditionally unorganized sector workers through **documented digital transactions**;
   c) improves **economic visibility** of previously informal work, helping workers build **verifiable employment records**.

However, its rise also has **negative implications**:
1. Lack of **job security** and **social protection** (health insurance, paid leave, retirement benefits) leaves workers **vulnerable to emergencies** (illness, accidents);
   b) constant **rating systems** and **algorithmic** **supervision** creates **unsustainable competitive pressures**, leading to increased worker **stress** and **burnout**.

2. a) **Irregular and unpredictable earnings** despite long working hours creates financial instability;
   b) **earnings disparities** emerge across platform workers, with significant variations based on platform type, location, and service category.

3. **Regulatory challenges** for governments in classifying gig workers appropriately, creating frameworks for taxation, and extending social security protections to this growing workforce segment.

Policymakers must **balance worker protection with innovation** by developing **regulations** that safeguard gig workers while maintaining economic flexibility. The **Rajasthan Platform-Based Gig Workers Act, 2023** and a similar **Karnataka** legislation represent important initial steps toward achieving this equilibrium. *(****366 words****)*

The economic reforms of 1991 entailed an **overhaul** of Indian economy in alignment with **market principles.** They marked a departure from the **post-independence economic model** (socialistic pattern, import substitution) towards the IMF-sponsored **Structural Adjustment Programme.**

**Need** for these reforms arose due to the following challenges:
1. Changing **global economic conditions** post the **decline of communism** in Eastern Europe (drop in aid available to developing countries). This forced a **rethink** on India’s own **economic strategy** (inspired by the Soviet model) and the need to align with the **emerging global economic consensus**.

2. **Balance of Payment crisis (1990-91)** caused due to trade imbalances, declining remittances post- Gulf war etc. This triggered a sharp **decline in the forex reserves** (import cover declined to 2-3 weeks).

3. a) Progressive deterioration in **macro-economic fundamentals** (fiscal deficit, persistent inflation) led to a **downgrade in credit ratings** (made external borrowing costlier);
   b) overhanging effects of post- independence economic policies **(industrial licensing, production controls, import substitution**) undermined India’s **economic competitiveness** (low technological base of manufacturing, subscale businesses).

4. a) Pervasive inefficiencies in the **public sector** due to lack of effective **competition** from the private sector;
   b) myriad **bureaucratic frameworks** (permissions, inspection) giving rise to problems like **rent-** **seeking, red-tapism**.

The key **objectives** of the economic reforms of 1991:
1. a) Resolution of BoP** situation through **foreign exchange reforms (**devaluation of rupee, rupee convertibility**);
   b) liberalization of trade policy** to enhance **competitiveness** of domestic manufacturing (new technologies) and develop India’s **consumer market.****

2. a) Macro-economic stabilization** by restoring **fiscal discipline** (containing government expenditure and raising revenue);**
   b) **rapid industrial development** through de-regulation (abolition of licensing requirements, removal of price controls).

3. a) Financial sector reforms** (private banks, FDI in banking) to expand the **resource base** of banks and **ease credit access**;**
   b) development of a broad-based **capital market** in the country (permitting foreign institutional investors);
   c) **strengthening capital market regulations** (statutory status to the SEBI).

4. a) Taxation reforms (**lower rates, procedural simplification) to encourage **savings** and promote **voluntary disclosure of income;****
   b) indirect tax reforms to create a **common national market** for goods and services.

5. Reducing the size of the ‘**bloated public sector’ (**privatization) and enhancing its **efficiency** (managerial autonomy).

The economic reforms of 1991 laid the foundation for India's transformation, but the journey towards a **'Viksit** **Bharat by 2047'** requires another package of reforms. **Reforms 2.0** (technology upgradation, digitization, land reforms, etc.) need to aim at achieving structural overhaul of India’s economy and be guided by the principles of **inclusivity, sustainability** and **dynamism.** ***(407 words)***

## Q8 [Year: 2026]
**Question:** Elaborate on the various policy instruments available to the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) for regulating  money supply in the economy. Also, explain why the RBI is known as the ‘lender of last resort’.
*Metadata: [Year: 2026] [Institute: Forum IAS] [Programme: MGP] [is_pyq: false] [Group: ] [Exam: ] [Stage: Mains] [Paper: Mains - GS 3] [Subject: Indian Economy] [Section Group: Macroeconomics & Fiscal Policy] [Micro Topic: Indian Economy and issues relating to planning, mobilization of resources, GDP, and monetary framework] [Sub Topic: Monetary policy] [Macro Tag: Descriptive, Analytical] [Micro Tag: Elaborate, Explain, RBI, Money supply]*
#### Answer from ForumIAS

| **Approach:** • Introduce the answer with a brief background about the RBI. <br>• In the body, elaborate on the various policy instruments available to RBI for regulating money supply in the economy. <br>• Next, explain why it is termed as the ‘lender of last resort’. <br>• Conclude by highlighting the significance of RBI in ensuring financial stability and safeguarding depositors' interests. |
| --- |

The Reserve Bank of India **(RBI)** is the **Central banking authority** in India, established under the **RBI Act, 1934.** Its **key objective**, among others, is to maintain **price stability** while **supporting economic growth**. Different **policy instruments** are available to the RBI for regulating money supply in the economy, as discussed:
1. **Quantitative measures** (alter the money supply in the economy directly)
   a) Altering **reserve ratios** like **CRR** (4% in Feb 2025), SLR (18% in Feb 2025) which impacts **cash** **in circulation** and **credit availability**. For instance, a reduction of CRR/ SLR is likely to boost money supply in the economy.

   b) i) **Liquidity Adjustment Facility** (LAF) instruments like **Repo (**6.25% in Feb 2025**), Reverse** **repo rates (**3.35% in Feb 2025) and **Standing Deposit Facility** (6% in Feb 2025). They primarily help the RBI maintain **short-term liquidity** by injecting or absorbing funds in the banking system.
       ii) **long-term** liquidity needs are met through **Long-Term Repo Operations (**LTROs**)** which provide 1-3-year loans to banks.

   c) Other mechanism like
   i) **Bank rate** (6.5% in Feb 2025) when **no collateral** is involved (lending occurs through discounting bills of exchange);
      ii) **Marginal Standing Facility** (6.5% in Feb 2025) when banks are short of securities above SLR limit;
      iii) **Open Market Operations** (to inject durable liquidity in the banking system (e.g. Operation Twist 2019) and **Market** **Stabilization Schemes.**

2. **Qualitative Measure**s (alter the money supply **indirectly**)

   a) i) **Credit rationing** by fixing limits to certain sectors, e.g. CRAR framework, Priority Sector Lending (PSL) norms;
       ii) varying **margin requirements** for different sectors.

   b) i) Regulation of **consumer credit** through adjusting minimum down payment and period of repayment;
       ii) **moral suasion** to make banks adhere to its policies and directives.

   c) **Direct action** like placing under Prompt Corrective Action (PCA) Framework (Allahabad Bank) or imposing **moratorium on lending (**Yes Bank).

RBI is termed as the '**lender of last resort**' because it can extend **credit to a bank** (that is solvent but faces temporary liquidity challenges) when **no one else is willing** to lend to it. For e.g., RBI extended Rs **60000-crore** **credit line** to **Yes Bank** during the 2020 crisis.

Thus, the RBI employs a **mix of quantitative and qualitative instruments** to regulate the money supply and ensure financial stability. As the **lender of last resort**, its role is to provide emergency **liquidity to solvent bank**s facing crises, safeguarding depositors’ interests and the banking system. ***(397 words)***

Over the last **6 years** (2019-2024), the Indian **Rupee has depreciated** at an **average annual rate of 3.3%**, recently breaching the **87/USD levels** (Jan-Feb 2025). This is **attributed** to both global (‘**Fed Tapering’, trade** **war, geo-political conflicts**) and domestic factors (**FII withdrawal, high inflation, poor corporate earnings**). Its multi-dimensional implications are:
1. a) Increased cost of **inelastic imports** (edible oil, crude oil);
   b) cascading effect on inflation (**imported** **inflation**) through impact across the supply chain (rise in transportation costs, essential raw materials);
   c) exacerbation of **current account deficit** (import dependency) leading to concerns about **forex outgo**;
   d) higher costs of **external debt servicing**.

2. a) Challenges for **monetary policy management** (limited scope for interest rate cuts amid inflation);
   b) decline in domestic **equity markets**, affecting investors across segments;
   c) higher **cost of living** **for Indian students abroad** and costlier **outbound travel**;
   d) potential deterioration of the **investment** **climate** (flight of capital).

3. a) Improved **export competitiveness** in the global markets (cheaper exports), benefitting specific sectors (**pharma, IT, gems and jewellery**);
   b) costlier imports encourage **import substitution,** **boosting local manufacturing**;
   c) **rise** in inward **remittances** due to favorable exchange rates, **increasing forex reserves**;
   d) raises India’s attraction as a **tourist destination** for **overseas travellers** (as makes travel cheaper).

**Following policy measures** can be adopted to stabilize the currency:
1. a) Frequent **direct market interventions by the RBI** to curb “**excessive volatility**” (dollar sell-off);
   b) adoption of a **tight monetary policy** (high interest rates to reduce money supply);
   c) incentivizing **Non-** **Resident Foreign Currency deposits** (exemption from CRR and SLR requirements);
   d) strategic **currency swaps** during periods of heavy depreciation.

2. a) Revising and **streamlining FPI regulations** to boost foreign investment in Indian **debt instruments** [SEBI (FPI) Regulations, 2019];
   b) easing **External Commercial Borrowing** norms to improve foreign debt inflows;
   c) promoting **rupee-denominated debt instruments** (Masala Bonds) to mitigate

exchange rate volatility (higher debt repayment).

3. a) Curbing imports of **non-essential goods** (Gold);
   b) promotion of **exports and diversification of** **trade** basket, especially for inelastic commodities (crude oil);
   c) maintaining **fiscal discipline** to curb **inflation** and raise investor confidence.

Despite its recent steep fall, the **Indian Rupee** continues to **outperform its peers** in the other emerging market economies (Philippines, Thailand). Also, the **high forex reserves** (~$654bn, March 2025) provides a **cushion for** **BoP** against exchange rate volatility. This reflects the **strong structural foundations** of the Indian economy despite significant currency depreciation. (***394 words***)

**Q.10. “India's economic trajectory over the last three decades reflects a decisive shift from a mixed** **economy to a market-oriented economy”. Comment.**

***Introduce the answer by mentioning the three broad categories of economies. In the body, highlight the*** ***characteristics that reflect a shift from a mixed economy to a market-oriented economy. Next, provide*** ***counterarguments. Conclude by emphasizing that India’s economic trajectory reflects a growing market*** ***orientation while retaining key mixed-economy features.***

**Economies** can be broadly classified into **three types** based on the extent of competition and government intervention viz. **Market Economy** (minimal government interference and driven by supply and demand), **Command Economy** (pre-dominant government control over production and distribution) and **Mixed Economy** (combines economic freedom with government intervention to achieve social and economic objectives)

**Post-independence**, India followed a **mixed-economy model**. However, in the last three decades, some experts argue that its economic trajectory reflects a decisive shift toward a market-oriented economy due to the following reasons:
1. a) Deregulation** of key sectors to enhance **private participation** (telecom, aviation);**
   b) **liberalization** of the **legal** and **regulatory framework** to promote **ease of doing business** (FEMA 1999, Competition Act 2002) .

2. a) **Raised** **FDI limits**, including opening up of hitherto **forbidden sectors** (space, nuclear energy, defence) for private and foreign participation;
   b) dedicated efforts towards **privatization** of government enterprises (Air India) and **closure of ‘sick’ PSUs** (Hindustan Cables).

3. a) Measures for **trade** **liberalization** (trade agreements);
   b) active efforts to **integrate with the global** **economy** (membership of IPEF);
   c) reforms in **currency market** (full convertibility on current account).

4. a) Greater emphasis on promoting **entrepreneurship** (Start-Up India) and **self-employment** (SHGs);
   b) changes in **government hiring practices** (lateral entry, rising contractual engagement as against full time employment);
   c) policy shift towards towards **lesser government control** (“minimum government, maximum governance”; **ES 2024-25** calls for ‘**systemic deregulation**’ ).

Despite the evident shift towards a market economy, India **continues to retain several features of a mixed** **economy,** as discussed:
1. a) Pre-dominant state presence** in key sectors of the economy [e.g., ONGC, CIL(energy)];**
   b) existence of **state monopolies** in specific sectors (Railway operations);
   c) persistent support for the **revival** of PSUs (BSNL package).

2. a) **Licensing requirements** that pose entry and exit barriers (banking, telecom);
   b) elaborate regulatory framework to prevent **monopoly** (CCI) and check **fraudulent** practices in the markets (various regulations of RBI and SEBI);
   c) ‘restrictive’ **land acquisition** and **labour policies** (FRA 2006, LARR 2013, labour laws).

3. a) **Protectionist trade policies** to safeguard the interests of domestic industries (import tariffs, didn’t join RCEP);
   b) **price control mechanisms** of essential commodities (buffer stocks of FCI, periodic DPCOs by NPPA);
   c) **gradual pace of reforms** across sectors due to **regulatory complexities** and **stakeholder concerns** (repealing of three farm laws).

4. a) Various **welfare schemes** to achieve socio-economic goals (Ayushman Bharat, MGNREGA, NSAP);
   b) **expansive fiscal policy** to promote **government investment** (housing, infrastructure);
   a) **government transfers** and **subsidies** to support citizens particularly the vulnerable groups (food subsidy, PM-KISAN).

India’s economic trajectory **reflects** a **growing market orientation** while **retaining key mixed-economy** **features.** This balance helps achieve economic growth, macroeconomic stability, and **social equity** in accordance with constitutional principles. (***442 words)***
