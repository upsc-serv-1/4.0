**Question:** Bring out the factors responsible for growing salience of Africa in India’s foreign policy framework. Also,  highlight the contemporary areas of their bilateral engagement.

#### Answer from ForumIAS

| **Approach:** • Introduce the answer with a brief background about the India-Africa relations. <br>• In the body, first bring out the factors responsible for the growing salience of Africa in India’s foreign policy framework. <br>• Next, highlight the contemporary areas of their bilateral engagement. <br>• Conclude by suggesting a way forward for the future. |
| --- |

India-Africa relations are **deep-rooted**, stemming from **ancient trade and cultural exchanges** across the Indian Ocean. These ties go **beyond strategic and economic interests**, as they are also founded on **solidarity** during **anti-** **colonial** and **post-independent** nation-building struggles.

In contemporary times, Africa has been **growing in salience** in India’s foreign policy framework due to the following factors:
1. a) Africa’s rich **natural resource** base and a youthful **demographic profile** (60% population below 25 years) offer **opportunities** for **complementary partnerships** and can support India’s economic growth and **energy security;**
   b) **similar experiences** as members of the developing world create opportunities for **"South-South cooperation"** (IBSA).

2. a) Strategic value of **Africa as the single largest bloc of nations** in plurilateral and multilateral fora (e.g. Climate Change summits, WTO) to achieve **common objectives;**
   b) enlisting Africa’s support for fulfilling **India’s global ambitions,** e.g., permanent seat in the UNSC;
   c) presence of large **Indian diaspora** (~3 million PIOs/NRIs) in Africa serves as a vital **socio-economic bridge**, strengthening **bilateral cultural and** **political ties.**

3. a) **Strategic balancing of China’s** growing footprint on the continent, prompting India to collaborate with other **like-minded countries** in the continent (e.g. Asia-Africa Growth Corridor with Japan);
   b) pressing **common** **developmental** (e.g. India-Africa Health Fund) and **security challenges** (maritime piracy off Somalia coast, Counter terrorism)**.**

In **contemporary times,** India-Africa **bilateral cooperation** encompasses a wide **variety of sectors** as listed below**:**
1. a) Democratising **institutions of global importance** (pivotal role in supporting the African Union's admission in G20);
   b) safeguarding **strategic autonomy** in a **polycrisis world** (non-alignment and pursuit of multipolarity);
   c) prioritizing the agenda of the **'Global South'** on various global platforms, (e.g. joint proposal for a TRIPS waiver on COVID-19 vaccines);
   d) **partnership** in global bodies (ISA, CDRI).

2. a) Growing cooperation with expansion of **strategic partnership** **agreements** (South Africa, Nigeria, Seychelles, Rwanda);
   b) **defence cooperation** encompassing joint development, military exercises (e.g., 'AFINDEX-23’), defence exports, security assistance;
   c) capacity development through ITEC programme and HICDPs;
   d) **maritime security** and counter terrorism (SAGAR vision).

3. a) Economic cooperation** with Africa being India’s **third** largest trading partner (USD 83.34 billion in 2023-24);**
   b) Indian **investments** span key African sectors such as telecommunication, pharmaceuticals, automotive, etc.;
   c) **development cooperation** including provision of **line of credit,** support for African students **(e.g. IIT-M campus** at Zanzibar);
   d) **technological cooperation** (e.g., e-Vidya Bharti Arogya Bharti (e-VBAB))**.**

The **ten guiding principles** enunciated by the Prime Minister in his address to the Ugandan Parliament (2018) offer the way forward to **pilot Indo-Africa engagement in the 21st century**. ***(395 words)***

The COVID-19 pandemic exposed the **West’s over-reliance on China for critical resources**. Also, the Russia- Ukraine conflict highlighted the alignment of **nations hostile to Western interests (**Russia-China-North Korea- Iran**)**. These events have positioned **India as a crucial player** in maintaining global power balance and countering China’s rising influence. The West is fostering India as **an alternative** to China in the following ways:
1. a) **China Plus One Strategy** encourages companies to diversify manufacturing locations due to **rising** **labor costs** in China, **trade tensions**, and the need for **supply chain resilience**
   b) shift to India due to its **large workforce** and growing **manufacturing capabilities** (e.g. over ₹1.5 trillion worth of iPhones exported from India in FY 2024- 25).

2. a) **Technological collaboration** through initiatives like the Initiative on Critical and Emerging Technologies **(iCET)**
   b) **India’s domestic policies** are also aiding in diversifying supply chains and reducing reliance on Chinese manufacturing. (e.g. PLI, Make in India).

3. a) **Friendshoring** - relocating supply chains to **political and economic allies** like India with shared values
   b) Build Back Better World **(B3W) Initiative** launched by G7 as an alternative to China's Belt and Road Initiative **(BRI)**
   c) India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor **(IMEC)** reducing reliance on Chinese routes and fostering economic ties.

**4.** a) Indo-Pacific Economic Framework **(IPEF)** focusing on trade, supply chain resilience, clean energy, and fair economy;
   b) **USA's Indo-Pacific Strategy** strengthening engagement to **counterbalance China's** influence and aggression;
   c) Formation of **Quad** (US, India, Japan, Australia) ensuring **a free and open** **Indo-Pacific** region.

5. a) Enhanced **defense cooperation** through joint military exercises (Malabar, Konkan, Varuna);
   b) **Blue Dot** **Network** mobilizing private sector investment as an alternative to China's BRI
   c) **QUAD's Supply-Chain** **Resilience Initiative** reducing China's dominance in the Indo-Pacific.

India should strive to enhance **infrastructure**, boost **financial inclusion**, strengthen **regulatory frameworks**, promote **innovation and skill development**, and adopt high environmental and social governance **(ESG) standards** to solidify its position as a key global economic player and strategic ally. ***(317 words)***

## Q3 [Year: 2026]
**Question:** “While the Gulf region is a lucrative destination, it also presents significant challenges to the Indian  diaspora.” Elaborate.
*Metadata: [Year: 2026] [Institute: Forum IAS] [Programme: MGP] [is_pyq: false] [Group: ] [Exam: ] [Stage: Mains] [Paper: Mains - GS 2] [Subject: International Relations] [Section Group: Global Geopolitics & Indian Diaspora] [Micro Topic: Indian Diaspora: Economic, Political & Social Impact of the Indian Diaspora] [Sub Topic: Indian Diaspora] [Macro Tag: Descriptive, Analytical, Applied] [Micro Tag: Elaborate, Gulf region, Indian diaspora]*
#### Answer from ForumIAS

| **Approach:** • Introduce the answer with a brief background about the extent and nature of the Indian diaspora in the Gulf region. <br>• In the body, first illustrate how the region is a lucrative destination for the Indian diaspora. <br>• Next, analyse how despite these opportunities, the Indian diaspora faces significant challenges in the Gulf region. <br>• Conclude by suggesting some government steps to address these challenges. |
| --- |

The Indian diaspora constitutes a large part of the population in the Gulf countries (e.g. ~21% in Kuwait). According to the **Ministry of External Affairs**, there are around **8 to 9 million Indians** in the Gulf region comprising both **white-** **collared** (doctors, engineers, IT experts, CAs) and **blue-collared** workers.

The following factors make the **Gulf region a lucrative destination** for the Indian diaspora:
1. a) **Historical links** (Arab traders) and **geographical proximity** facilitating regular visits to homeland;
   b) growing **bilateral trade relations** (exceeding **$100 billion annually**) creating opportunities for Indian entrepreneurs to serve as **intermediaries**;
   c) emergence of **successful Indian business conglomerates** (Lulu Group, GEMS Education) demonstrating the potential for wealth creation.

2. a) Higher level of **social and economic mobility** compared to India;
   b) presence of **vibrant Indian** **communities** and cultural associations providing familiar social environments;
   c) ample **connectivity** **with India**, including **simple visa process**;
   d) **recent initiatives** encouraging migration (UAE Golden Visa).

3. a) **High demand** for both **white-collar (**engineering, medical) and **blue-collar jobs (**construction workers) ;
   b) high **wage differential** with India;
   c) **stable job profile** (long term contracts);
   d) **increasing** **employment opportunities** due to the **recent efforts** at economic diversification (Saudi Vision 2030).

4. a) World class **physical** (airports, hospitals) and **social** (health, education) **infrastructure**;
   b) **business-** **friendly policies** (near-tax free regimes e.g., Dubai);
   c) avenues to maintain **work life balance** (entertainment economy);
   d) greater propensity to make wealth domestically by sending **remittances**.

However, despite these lucrative opportunities, the Indian diaspora in the Gulf regions faces the following **challenges**:
1. a) Fluid **political and security situation** in the region due to **terrorism, geo-political rivalries (**e.g. Gaza conflict);
   b) migrants are often **caught in the crossfire** (e.g., abduction of Indian nurses in Iraq) and need to be **rescued** out of conflict zones (e.g., **Operation Rahat** in Yemen);
   c) unavailability of **basic** **political** rights with arbitrary confiscation of **passports** and denial of **consular access**.

2. a) The **kafala (sponsorship) system** in most Gulf countries ties workers to specific employers, creating potential for **exploitation and abuse**;
   b) issues of **workplace safety** (e.g., Kuwait fire accident) and occupational health risks (e.g., FIFA- 2022, Qatar, labour issues);
   b) **discriminatory policies** favouring local workers **(Nitaqat system).**

3. a) **Conservative social norms** and **restrictive practices** limiting personal freedoms and expression;
   b) experiences of **racial discrimination** and **social stratification** based on nationality;
   c) **limited social**

**integration** with local populations due to cultural differences and institutional barriers; d) challenges in **preserving cultural identity** while respecting local customs and regulations.

Overcoming these challenges requires government intervention in the direction of **registration of employment** **agents,** **skill development**, **pre-departure orientation training** and **responsive grievance redressal** **mechanisms**. ***(427 words)***

## Q4 [Year: 2026]
**Question:** Explain the funding mechanisms of WHO and examine their implications for global health priorities. How  can developing nations contribute to making WHO more inclusive and effective?
*Metadata: [Year: 2026] [Institute: Forum IAS] [Programme: MGP] [is_pyq: false] [Group: ] [Exam: ] [Stage: Mains] [Paper: Mains - GS 2] [Subject: International Relations] [Section Group: International Organizations] [Micro Topic: International Institutions: Structure, Mandate & Functioning of Important Agencies & Fora] [Sub Topic: United Nations and its Agencies] [Macro Tag: Descriptive, Analytical, Applied] [Micro Tag: Explain, Examine, WHO, Developing nations]*
#### Answer from ForumIAS

| **Approach:** • Introduce the answer by providing a brief background of the World Health Organization. <br>• In the body, first discuss the funding mechanisms of WHO, then examine their implications for global health priorities. <br>• Next, explore how developing nations can contribute to making WHO more inclusive and effective. <br>• Conclude by highlighting the significance of a reformed WHO funding structure for global health governance. |
| --- |

World Health Organization (WHO) is a **specialized agency of the United Nations** that coordinates responses to **international public health issues** and emergencies. It also serves as the primary body for **global health** **governance**. However, the recent **U.S. withdrawal** in 2025 has **exposed structural vulnerabilities** in its funding model and decision-making processes. It prompts a critical reassessment of its **funding mechanisms**, as explored below:
1. a) **Assessed contributions (AC)** are **mandatory annual** membership fees determined by a formula based on a country’s GDP and ability to pay;
   b) these contributions provide **assured funding** for WHO's core operations (salaries of regular staff, day-to-day functioning).

2. a) **Voluntary contributions (VC)** from member states, donor agencies, and private organizations now constitute **over 80% of WHO's total budget**;
   b) these funds are typically **time-bound**, **linked to specific** **activities** (e.g. Polio elimination), and inherently **unpredictable**.

3. a) **Innovative financing mechanisms,** beyond the usual dual system, such as the **WHO Foundation** have been established to diversify funding sources;
   b) the foundation facilitates contributions from the **general** **public, individual major donors and corporate partners** to deliver on high-impact programmes.

These funding mechanisms have profound **implications for global health priorities**:
1. a) High-income countries and organisations exert **disproportionate influence** through funding (donor- driven agenda), creating skewed priorities (prioritizes high-visibility diseases);
   b) **vertical funding** results in neglect of **diseases affecting poorer regions** and they get priority only when they threaten wealthy nations (mPox in 2022);
   c) **vital medical resources** remain inequitably distributed (e.g. limited availability of mPox vaccine in DRC, while liberally offered in US).

2. a) **Earmarked funding** directed toward **specific programs** or initiatives restricts **WHO's financial** **autonomy** i.e., to allocate resources independently;
   b) **over-reliance on expertise** from select high- income countries creates vulnerability when personnel are recalled (US withdrawal);
   c) **short-term** **commitments** create challenges for long-term planning.

3. a) **Private sector partnerships** raise concerns about **potential conflicts of interest** and **undue influence** on policy decisions;
   b) **bilateral funding arrangements** between donors and recipient countries

sometimes **bypass WHO frameworks**, undermining its coordinating role; c) the rise of **hyper-nationalism** and "**nation first**" political movements in many countries has eroded the fabric of **global solidarity**, weakening **collective responses.**

**Developing nations**, can contribute to making WHO more **inclusive and effective** through:
1. a) **Collective funding initiatives** among global south nations to **supplement WHO funding gaps**, utilising regional platforms (e.g., BRICS);
   b) scaling up **domestic health financing** to reduce dependence on external donors;
   c) **pooling resources** at regional levels to create **sustainable funding streams** (e.g. Africa CDC).

2. a) **Investing in training experts** specifically in global health to develop **indigenous expertise** that can be shared internationally;
   b) **redistributing specific divisions** to regional offices to focus WHO's work on geographies where attention is most needed (Africa and Asia);
   c) **streamlining administrative structures** to enhance organizational **efficiency and responsiveness.**

The landmark **WHO Pandemic Agreement of April 2025** exemplifies how **reformed global health institutions** with **balanced representation** can effectively address **contemporary challenges** through **mutual cooperation**. ***(465*** ***words)***

## Q7 [Year: 2026]
**Question:** Critically analyse India's evolving diplomatic, economic and strategic relations with the Central Asian  Republics (CARs) highlighting their increasing significance in regional and global geopolitics.
*Metadata: [Year: 2026] [Institute: Forum IAS] [Programme: MGP] [is_pyq: false] [Group: ] [Exam: ] [Stage: Mains] [Paper: Mains - GS 2] [Subject: International Relations] [Section Group: Global Geopolitics & Indian Diaspora] [Micro Topic: Groupings & Agreements: Bilateral, Regional & Global Groupings & Agreements Involving or Affecting India's Interests] [Sub Topic: Groupings involving Immediate and Extended neighbours] [Macro Tag: Analytical, Descriptive, Applied] [Micro Tag: Critically analyse, Highlighting, Central Asian Republics, India]*
#### Answer from ForumIAS

| **Approach:** • Introduce the answer with a brief background about CARs. <br>• In the body, first elaborate upon the evolving diplomatic, economic and strategic relations between India and CARs. <br>• Next, highlight the challenges in deeper engagement. <br>• Conclude with summarizing the benefits of engagement with CARs. |
| --- |

CARs are a **group of five nations** (Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan and Uzbekistan) that disintegrated from the erstwhile USSR in 1992. India considers the CARs as a part of her “**extended** **neighbourhood”** and engages with them through the **4Cs approach** i.e. Commerce, Connectivity, Contact and Capacity enhancement.

India’s **evolving diplomatic, economic and strategic relations** with the CARs is visible in efforts such as:
1. a) The launch of **dedicated policy** (“Connect Central Asia”, 2012) for consistent engagement;
   b) revitalizing India’s deep **historical and cultural ties** (trade through Silk Road, Buddhism connection, influence of Bollywood);
   c) through high-level **political exchange initiatives** like the India-Central Asia Dialogue.

2. a) **Cultural exchange programs** (e.g. annual Youth Delegation Visits);
   b) better **people to people** **interaction** (115% jump in Indians travelling to Uzbekistan in 2023);
   c) engaging  on **multilateral platforms** [member of SCO, observer status in the Eurasian Economic Union (EAEU)].

3. a) Enhanced **energy cooperation** benefitting from Central Asia’s rich natural resources (gold, oil, gas, Uranium) through **bilateral agreements** (e.g. with Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan for uranium supply) and **multilateral projects** (TAPI pipeline).

4. a) Boosting **trade and connectivity** with initiatives like the INSTC and the development of the Chabahar Port in Iran (to overcome land-route constraints of movement via Pakistan);
   a) **diversified economic** **engagement** in sectors like **pharmaceuticals** (e.g. export of generic medicine, training workshops) and **IT** (providing e-governance solutions).

5. a) **Security cooperation** through **joint military exercises**, such as KAZIND with Kazakhstan; **counter-** **terrorism collaborations** (e.g. RATS);
   b) enhanced strategic depth through establishment of **military** **bases** in Tajikistan (Farkhor and Gissar).

However, a number of **challenges** hinder deeper engagement. They are as follows:
1. a) Lack of direct **land connectivity,** compounded by **instability in Afghanistan** and Pakistan's **denial of** **transit**;
   b) limited progress on **alternative channels of connectivity (**e.g. INSTC, Ashgabat Corridor, TAPI pipeline).

2. a) Deep levels of **Chinese engagement**, along with India’s limitations in matching the **financing capacity** of China in the region (e.g. BRI);

However, it continues to remain **heavily reliant on imports** (from US, China, Germany etc) for sustaining the ecosystem. This exposes India’s scientific and technological enterprise to **tariff and other trade** **barriers** (e.g. imposition of a ‘**discounted reciprocal tariff’** (26%) on **Indian imports into the US).** Apart from the impact on **trade balance** and **industrial competitiveness**, it has several **possible implications** on India’s scientific and technical enterprise as discussed:

   b) **diversification of** **import sources** (e.g. Japan, South Korea, Israel) and leveraging **multi-lateral networks** (e.g. BRICS, SCO) to reduce over-dependence on the US.

2. a) Insulating scientific and technical research inputs from general-purpose tariffs or trade retaliation mechanisms (e.g. advocating their inclusion as **global public goods under WTO framework**);
   b) **streamlining domestic procedures** (e.g. custom clearances) for research-related equipment.

3. Encouraging **talent mobility arrangements** with like-minded countries to create **mutual dependencies** and benefits. This will act as an **insurance against reckless trade policies** .

Instead of knee jerk reactions to trade barriers, India should use it as an opportunity to **build long-term scientific** **resilience**, rooted in **indigenous innovation,** **global collaboration**, and **strategic autonomy**. It helps us in realizing the aspiration to become the **global knowledge hub by 2047**. (***438 words***)
