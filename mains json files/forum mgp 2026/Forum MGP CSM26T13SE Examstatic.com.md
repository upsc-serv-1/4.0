**Question:** What do you understand by agroecology? Identify and discuss its key principles.

Approach - Introduce the answer with a brief description of agroecology. In the body, identify and discuss its key

principles with relevant examples. Conclude by mentioning the significance of agroecology for the future.

Agroecology is an integrated approach that incorporates ecological principles and social equity in agriculture and

food systems with the goal to build greater resilience,resource-use efficiency and overall sustainability.

The principles of agroecology incorporate ecological, social, and economic factors into food production and

consumption. Some of the key principles are discussed below.

1. a) Recycling: Use of local renewable resources, closing nutrient and biomass cycles (as far as possible) ;

   b) Input reduction: reduce or eliminate dependency on synthetic/external inputs (e.g. Zero Budget Natural

Farming); c) Soil health: securing and enhancing soil health by managing organic matter and increasing soil

biological activity for improved plant growth (e.g. Soil Health Card); d) Animal health: ensuring animal

health and welfare (e.g. National Livestock Mission).

2. a) Biodiversity: Maintaining and enhancing species diversity, functional diversity and genetic resources

within the agroecosystem (Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers Rights Act); b) Synergy: Enhancing

positive ecological interaction, synergy, and complementarity amongst the elements of agroecosystems

(plants, animals, trees, soil, water).

3. a) Economic diversification: Diversifying on-farm incomes, providing small-scale farmers with greater

financial independence (e.g. integrated farming systems); b) co-creation of knowledge: co-creation and

horizontal sharing of knowledge including local and scientific innovation, especially through farmer-to-

farmer exchange (e.g. NADEP* composting); c) social values and diets: build food systems based on

culture, identity, tradition, social and gender equity of local communities that provide healthy, diversified,

seasonally and culturally appropriate diets.

4. a)Fairness: Supporting dignified and robust livelihoods, especially small-scale food producers, based on

fair trade, fair employment and fair treatment of intellectual property rights; b) connectivity: developing

fair and short distribution networks and re-embedding food systems into local economies.

5. a) Land and natural resource governance: recognizing and supporting family farmers, smallholders etc as

sustainable managers and guardians of natural and genetic resources; b) Participation: social organization

and greater participation in decision-making to support decentralized governance and local adaptive

management of agricultural and food systems (e.g. FPOs).

Agroecology offers a sustainable alternative to the challenges of industrial agriculture, including food insecurity,

climate change, and social inequality. A gradual, evidence-based adoption of agroecological practices—without

disrupting current levels of food production—presents a balanced path forward. (326 words)

*NADEP: Narayan Deotao Pandharipande method of composting

The Digital Agriculture Mission is an umbrella scheme to support various digital agriculture initiatives. The scheme is built on two foundational pillars- Agri Stack and the Krishi Decision Support System (KDSS). It also includes  ‘Soil Profile Mapping’ and aims to enable farmer-centric digital services to provide timely and reliable information for the agriculture sector. It can benefit the agricultural sector in following ways:
1. a) Improved delivery of government benefits and services to farmers quickly, efficiently (geo-referenced farmland maps) and transparently (easier authentication of farmers through unique farmer IDs). It will be done through a federated registry of all farmers (identification of farmers through minimal demographic information, linkage with farmland plot records);
   b) streamlining of all farmer related information may eliminate the need to liaison with separate departments and the challenges (delays, inaccuracy) related to physical records.

2. a) Spin off benefits (easy access for Agri-techs to onboard farmers, allows financial institutions to assess the creditworthiness of farmers);
   b) enables data driven decision-making and real time monitoring (agro- climate based cropping patterns, early warning for pest attacks and disasters), lowering the risks associated with agricultural operations;
   c) Unified Service Interface that brings together various stakeholders (farmers, government agencies, agri-tech companies, financial institutions) can facilitate collaboration for a more sustainable agricultural system.

3. a) Access to high quality data drives agricultural innovation (Agristack sandbox) and knowledge sharing;
   b) smooth and more efficient methods of crop survey (using smartphones and drones) facilitates timely interventions (seeds, fertilizers, pesticides, farm machinery) and better response (credit, technical advice);
   c) addresses crop- specific issues that are customized to the needs of individual farmers (remote sensing data under KDSS);
   d) enables informed and convenient access to markets for farmers.

4. a) Better awareness of soil health through detailed soil profile maps for ~142 million hectares of agricultural land;
   b) facilitates improved planning and resource allocation through Digital General Crop Estimation Survey, aimed at enhancing the accuracy of crop yield estimates;
   c) enables better delivery of crop insurance (especially post disaster);
   d) optimization of agricultural value chains through tailored, crop- specific advisory services customized to individual farmers' needs.

However, its implementation faces challenges such as privacy concerns, rural digitization gaps, and monopolization risks. Addressing these through a robust data protection framework, stakeholder coordination and closing digitization gaps is key to building a sustainable agricultural sector that empowers farmers and strengthens national food security. (415 words)

## Q3 [Year: 2026]
**Question:** “Water is life, water is food.” In this context, explain the importance of sustainable water management in Indian agriculture.
*Metadata: [Year: 2026] [Institute: Forum IAS] [Programme: MGP] [is_pyq: false] [Group: ] [Exam: ] [Stage: Mains] [Paper: Mains - GS 3] [Subject: AGRICULTURE] [Section Group: Agriculture & Farm Dynamics] [Micro Topic: Irrigation design, management, water efficiency, and conservation infrastructure] [Sub Topic: Water Conservation] [Macro Tag: Descriptive, Applied] [Micro Tag: Explain, Sustainable water management, Indian agriculture]*
#### Answer from ForumIAS

| **Approach:** • Introduce the answer by providing some data related to water use in agriculture. <br>• In the body, first explain the importance of sustainable water management in Indian agriculture. <br>• Next, highlight the steps taken by the government in this regard. <br>• Conclude by underlining the importance of shifting from “land productivity” to “water productivity”. |
| --- |

According to the Economic Survey, India's water productivity (crop output per unit water used) is significantly lower. It is 2-4 times more than countries like China and Brazil, with ~80% of freshwater withdrawals diverted to agriculture. This reflects the importance of sustainable water management in Indian agriculture, as discussed below:
1. a) Mismatch between population and freshwater availability (~18% of the world’s population, <4% of fresh water resources);
   b) challenges to freshwater availability posed by pollution (e.g. Yamuna toxic foam) and encroachment (e.g. Bellandur lake Bengaluru);
   c) declining groundwater reserves (e.g. Punjab) due to heavy use (~87% of groundwater extracted used in agriculture- CGWB);
   d) competition with industrial and domestic sectors driven by increasing need for industrialization and a growing population.

2. a) ‘Water-blind’ cropping patterns (e.g. sugarcane cultivation in Marathwada) that impact long-term agricultural sustainability;
   b) vagaries of climate change (unpredictability of monsoon, increased heat- stress) and the need to climate-proof agriculture;
   c) alleviating stress over shared water resources (e.g. inter-state rivers) to resolve conflicts around them;
   d) need to reduce ‘gendered-impacts’ of water scarcity (e.g. Maharashtra’s water wives).

3. a) Improved crop yields (20-30%) as a result of precision irrigation ( direct water delivery to roots), boosting food security;
   b) reduced soil moisture evaporation that prevents salinization;
   c) lesser probability of water pollution from agriculture run-off (e.g. eutrophication);
   d) prevents “water export” and curbs GHG emissions (e.g. CH4 release from flooded paddy fields), lowering the ecological footprint of agriculture.

4. a) Reduced energy consumption in agricultural operations (e.g. electricity for operating water pump sets);
   b) inhibited weed and fungal growth (reduced soil moisture) lowers chemical usage in agriculture;
   c) overall reduction in cost of cultivation (less labour required for land levelling) and drudgery for the farmer (e.g. automated micro-irrigation systems).

A paradigmatic shift from “land productivity” to “water productivity” is required to overcome the water stress being faced by Indian agriculture. It can be achieved through technological interventions (Digital Agricultural Mission), policy reforms (e.g. National Water Policy 2012), and community participation (e.g. Catch the Rain campaign, Sahi Fasal campaign) to build a water-secure and food-secure future. (354 words)

## Q4 [Year: 2026]
**Question:** Examine the role of Primary Agricultural Credit Societies in uplifting rural communities and promoting inclusive economic growth in the country.
*Metadata: [Year: 2026] [Institute: Forum IAS] [Programme: MGP] [is_pyq: false] [Group: ] [Exam: ] [Stage: Mains] [Paper: Mains - GS 3] [Subject: AGRICULTURE] [Section Group: Agriculture & Farm Dynamics] [Micro Topic: Storage, transport, and marketing of agricultural produce along with digital e-technology interventions] [Sub Topic: Tech in Agriculture Credit] [Macro Tag: Analytical, Applied] [Micro Tag: Examine, Primary Agricultural Credit Societies, India]*
#### Answer from ForumIAS

| **Approach:** • Introduce the answer with a definition of PACS. <br>• In the body, first examine the role of PACS in uplifting rural communities and promoting inclusive economic growth in the country. <br>• Next, highlight the limitations of PACS in doing the same, followed by listing some recent government efforts for expanding the PACS network. <br>• Conclude with importance of PACS in line with the Sahkar se Samriddhi vision. |
| --- |

PACS are the grass-root level arms of the short-term co-operative credit structure. They deal directly with the rural (agricultural) borrowers, disburse loans and collect repayments of loans given and some undertake distribution and marketing functions. They play a critical role in uplifting rural communities and promoting inclusive growth in the country in the following ways:
1. a) Facilitate the flow of institutional credit (from RBI/NABARD via StCBs/DCCBs to the ultimate borrowers);
   b) formal lending source for the rural population improving financial inclusion and bridge rural-urban divide; serving the underserved (small and marginal farmers received 59.6% of total loans disbursed in 2020-21);
   c) highly affordable rate (effective interest rate of ~4%);
   d) better accessibility (credit with minimum paperwork);
   e) providing doorstep financial services (PACS as Bank Mitra equipped with Micro ATMs).

2. a) Provide credit for allied activities (animal husbandry, fisheries) and diversification of rural livelihood facilitating broader economic development;
   b) support mechanization and technology adoption;
   c) promote rural entrepreneurship and increase in agricultural productivity;
   d) enhance collective bargaining power of small and marginal farmers (encouraging formation of FPOs) and forward and backward linkages (market access, processing facility);
   e) build social capital by transforming agriculture  into a collective activity.

3. a) Human resource development through training and capacity building (best agricultural practices, financial management, marketing etc.);
   b) facilitate access to generic medicine (PACS as Jan Aushadhi Kendras);
   c) provision for quality and affordable agri-input and services (traditional inputs through PMKSK, bio-organic fertilizers, diesel/petrol);
   d) access to off-grid electricity (convergence with PM-KUSUM).
   e) As member-owned institutions, they operate on the principles of cooperative governance, giving rural people a stake in their own development.

4. a) Promote savings culture (through various savings schemes);
   b) contribute to water security (PACS as Paani Samiti) by overseeing implementation of Piped Water Supply projects in rural locales (additional employment opportunity);
   c) strengthen e-governance by providing more than 300 e-services (PACS as nodal points for CSC services).

However, they suffer from certain deficiencies, as discussed below:
1. a) Iniquitous credit distribution between states (more than 20% societies located in Maharashtra alone) and wide inter-district credit disparities ( Rs.14000 per hectare in Banda district whereas Rs.186000 per hectare in Meerut district);
   b) continued reliability of small and marginal farmers on informal lending (only 40% covered by formal credit);
   c) dysfunctional PACS (more than 12000 PACS dormant or defunct, NAFSCOB);
   d) reduced share in rural credit (less than 12% from ~45% two decades ago);
   e) large scale of non-repayment of loans and mounting dues (with ~50% NPAs) raises doubts about their long-term sustainability (less than 50% of them are profitable, RBI report);
   f) limited lending capacity owing to low deposit base .

2. a) Lack of proper infrastructure (office building, banking counters, furniture, safe, godowns etc.) leading to poor visibility;
   b) governance inefficiencies due to political influence (due to dependence on government subsidies);
   c) prevailing corruption (e.g. recent crop loan waiver irregularities in Telangana);
   d) limited technological adoption (not connected to Core Banking Solutions);
   e) severe under-staffing (an average of only 1.57 staff per PACS, Vaidyanathan Committee (2005)).

To address these limitations and fully exploit the developmental potential of PACS the government is making continuous efforts to transform these credit societies into a multi-service center (MSC).  UN’s declaration of 2025 as the International Year of Cooperatives emphasizes the significance in expanding the PACS in line with the Sahakar se Samriddhi vision. (491 words)

Nuclear technology refers to the applications of nuclear reactions and the principles of nuclear physics for various purposes, including energy production, medical treatments, and scientific research. It holds immense potential to be leveraged in enhancing food security and promoting agricultural sustainability, as discussed below:
1. a) Development of new crop varieties (plant mutation breeding) to enhance beneficial variations (tolerance to herbicides, higher yield potential, improved nutritional quality, enhanced stress tolerance);
   b) improvement of agriculture water management through soil moisture content monitoring (Neutron soil moisture meters (neutron probe), water flux study (cosmic ray neutron probe);

   b) nutrition programming to assess diet quality and micronutrient absorption (nutritional security);

   d) seafood safety detecting POPs (receptor binding assay technology);
   e) nuclear energy can be a reliable source of electricity for mechanization across agricultural value chain.

1. a) Negative public perception towards nuclear technology restricting wider acceptance;
   b) radiation safety and protection (safe handling);
   c) waste disposal (radioactive waste);
   d) insufficient skilled labour availability.

2. a) Limited infrastructure (only 25 food irradiation facilities in India);
   b) competition from conventional and alternative technology (biotechnology);
   c) stringent regulations and licensing requirements;
   d) concerns about the management of nuclear waste.

The Atoms4Food initiative provides the framework to strengthen international collaboration in leveraging nuclear technology and achieving the targets under SDG 2. (334 words)

## Q6 [Year: 2026]
**Question:** Despite its enormous significance, the Food Processing Industry (FPI) in the country is still underdeveloped. Discuss the statement and suggest corrective measures.

#### Answer from ForumIAS

| **Approach:** • Introduce the answer by giving a brief background of food processing and its enormous significance. <br>• In the body, discuss the reasons for the underdeveloped status of the food processing industry in the country. <br>• Next, suggest corrective measures. <br>• Conclude by highlighting the opportunities for the growth of the sector and recent measures for the same. |

Food Processing refers to the conversion of raw edible products from agriculture, animal husbandry, fisheries etc., into high value-added products through industrial processes. It has enormous significance in India as it provides vital linkages and synergies between the two pillars of the economy, i.e. agriculture and industry. While FPI is a sunrise sector of the economy (GVA increased from 1.61 lakh cr in 2015-16 to 1.92 lakh cr in 2022-23- MoSPI), it is still under-developed in the country due to following inhibiting factors:
1. a) Time-sensitive nature of agri-produce and vagaries of weather make the availability of consistent quality raw materials difficult. E.g., price volatility of tomatoes and potatoes may affect ketchup and chips industries;
   b) infrastructure gaps such as poor roads, inadequacy of the cold chain network etc. cause supply chain disruptions. E.g., annual post-harvest losses are close to Rs 90,000 crore (NITI Aayog report).

2. a) Huge concentration of unorganized segments (almost 75% firms across all product categories) leading to inefficiency, poor quality and hygiene standards. It also creates hurdles in scalability;
   b) poor forward and backward linkages due to supply chain fragmentation;
   c) low levels of technology adoption and innovation (e.g. food grade packaging) in the production process

3. a) Issues of ease of doing business such as bureaucratic red-tapes and tedious licensing policy hindering the establishment of FPIs (e.g., large number of forms associated with FSSAI licenses);
   b) cultural preferences in India where people prefer homemade/fresh food rather than processed/canned food has made it difficult for the FPI to thrive.

In order to give a boost to the FPIs, there is a need to take certain corrective measures, such as:
1. a) Establish risk sharing mechanisms through private sector participation, fiscal incentives and partnership models to create infrastructure for logistics, storage and processing.  Pradhan Mantri Kisan Sampada Yojana (PMKSY) is a welcome step;
   b) promote cluster-based development through schemes like mega food parks and setting up agro-industrial clusters.

2. a) Developing regulatory framework for contract and corporate farming;
   b) village-level procurement centres for perishables such as fruits, vegetables and dairy should be increased. E.g., upgradation of Gramin Agriculture Markets (GrAMS);
   c) providing last mile cold chain connectivity (e.g. reefer trucks);
   d) 

Single Window Clearance Systems for ease of doing business and simplified food safety compliance norms for small-scale processors.

3. a) Encourage academia and industry to commence courses in food packing, processing, biotechnology for constant supply of skilled manpower;
   b) building export facilitation centers and upgrading food labs to meet global standards;
   c) positioning Indian processed foods through GI tagging, branding, and digital platforms;
   d) tackling misleading information about processed foods (19 member committee constituted for the same in Feb 2025).

India’s status as a major producer of food (second largest producer of rice and vegetables) provides convergence for growth of agriculture and FPI. Recent budgetary measures (e.g. Makhana board in Bihar, National Institute of Food Technology, Entrepreneurship and Management) are likely to go a long way in realizing the potential of food processing as a “force for grassroots transformation”. (494 words)

## Q7 [Year: 2026]

#### Answer from ForumIAS

| **Approach:** • Introduce the answer by providing the status of the irrigation coverage in India. <br>• In the body, discuss the major challenges faced by the Indian irrigation system in recent times. <br>• Next, state the measures taken by the government for efficient irrigation management. <br>• Conclude by emphasizing the need for a multipronged approach to increase the efficiency of irrigation systems. |

According to the Economic Survey 2024-25, the coverage of irrigated area increased between FY16 and FY21 from 49.3% to 55% of the gross cropped area. 40% of the total irrigated area is watered through canal networks and the remaining through groundwater. Despite the expansion in area under coverage, Indian irrigation system is facing the following challenges in recent times:
1. a) Fluctuations in reservoir levels and water flow in irrigation channels due to unpredictability of monsoon
   b) gap between Irrigation Potential Created (IPC) and Irrigation Potential Utilized (IPU) [low efficiency of major and medium irrigation projects (~38%)];
   c) inequity in water distribution over the length of the canal system leading to “tailenders’ deprivation”.

2. a) Challenges of water use efficiency (e.g. flood irrigation) posing concerns about over exploitation of groundwater and soil salinization (due to waterlogging of soil);
   b) low adoption rates of micro-irrigation systems (high cost of installation, cultural factors);
   c) poor maintenance of irrigation infrastructure (leakages, siltation, evaporation) leading to water loss;
   d) wasteful usage of water due to ‘perverse subsidies’ (free electricity, water) and legal gaps (provisions of Indian Easement Act).

3. a) Regional disparity in the development of irrigation networks (more developed in northern parts of the country);
   b) inter-state river water disputes;
   c) reduction in availability of clean water (river pollution, decline in e-flow);
   d) threat of climate change (altered rainfall patterns) affects scientific management of irrigation resources.

4. a) Time and cost overruns in major irrigation projects (recent CAG report on delays in executing irrigation projects in Telangana);
   b) hurdles in land acquisition, environmental clearance for irrigation projects;
   c) 

cascading effects of major irrigation projects (alleged flooding in Bengal due to Damodar valley project, environmental impact on Panna tiger reserve from Ken-Betwa link project).

The government has taken several measures for efficient irrigation management:
1. a) Prioritization of major/medium irrigation projects under PMKSY-AIBP;
   b) enhancing utilization of irrigation potential created and participatory irrigation management (Pani Panchayat) under CADWM;
   c) groundwater management and aquifer-rejuvenation to check the declining groundwater levels (Atal Bhujal Yojana).

2. a) PMKSY-HKKP-GW scheme to provide financial assistance to States for assured ground water irrigation to small and marginal farmers;
   b) Per Drop More Crop scheme to focus on water use efficiency at farm level through micro-irrigation (Drip and Sprinkler Irrigation System);
   c) MIF under NABARD has been created to help states in mobilizing resources.

3. a) Sahi Fasal campaign under the National Water Mission. It seeks to nudge farmers in water-stressed areas to grow crops suited to the agro-climatic-hydro characteristics of the area;
   b) decentralized irrigation management through OFWM practices.

4. a) CWC has undertaken the SIMP to modernize Major / Medium Irrigation projects for improving water use efficiency and increasing crop water productivity. This will ultimately help in increasing farmer’s income in the command area of the projects through application of national / international best practices.

Thus, a multipronged approach that integrates policy reform, technological innovation, and community participation is crucial for enhancing the efficiency of India’s irrigation systems and promoting sustainable agriculture. (474 words)

## Q8 [Year: 2026]
**Question:** “The agricultural subsidy regime in the country needs reforms to address the distortions it causes.” Comment.
*Metadata: [Year: 2026] [Institute: Forum IAS] [Programme: MGP] [is_pyq: false] [Group: ] [Exam: ] [Stage: Mains] [Paper: Mains - GS 3] [Subject: AGRICULTURE] [Section Group: Agriculture & Farm Dynamics] [Micro Topic: Issues related to direct and indirect farm subsidies and minimum support prices (MSP)] [Sub Topic: Agriculture Subsidy] [Macro Tag: Analytical, Applied] [Micro Tag: Comment, Agricultural subsidy, Reforms, India]*
#### Answer from ForumIAS

| **Approach:** • Introduce by defining agriculture subsidies. <br>• In the body, first highlight the distortions caused by the agricultural subsidy regime in the country. <br>• Next, suggest comprehensive reforms to address these consequences. <br>• Conclude by underlining the need for increasing capital investments in agriculture. |
| --- |

Agricultural subsidies are government incentives paid to farmers, agri-businesses, agricultural organizations to supplement their income and influence the cost and supply of agricultural commodities. They constitute about 2% of India’s GDP and include both direct (e.g. PM KISAN) and indirect subsidies (e.g. subsidy on farm inputs). Though beneficial, agriculture subsidy regime in the country causes the following distortions:
1. a) Consequences for budget management (increase in revenue deficit);
   b) encourages resource-intensive farming leading to resource inefficiency (e.g. unabated power and irrigation subsidy → lower water-use efficiency);
   c) causes diversion of resources away from productivity enhancement measures i.e. opportunity cost of subsidies  (e.g. land improvement, research).

2. a) Distort market signals, leading to overproduction of certain crops (e.g., rice, wheat) at the cost of others (e.g. pulses, oilseeds);
   b) prone to leakages (e.g., diversion of subsidized urea) and corruption;
   c) issues with beneficiary targeting (generally reach landowning, big farmers).

3. a) Skewing of cropping patterns in conflict with agro-climatic conditions (e.g. paddy cultivation in Haryana and Punjab, sugarcane cultivation in Marathwada region);
   b) impact on agricultural biodiversity due to neglect of indigenous varieties and monoculture practices.

4. a) Depletion (groundwater) and pollution (biomagnification, soil salinization) of natural resources;
   b) 

increased carbon footprint of agriculture (e.g. CH4  emissions from flooded paddy fields).

The following comprehensive reforms are required to address the distortion caused by them:
1. a) Reform MSP procurement to align with agro-climatic realities favouring climate-resilient crops like millets (jowar, bajra, ragi) at their MSP ;
   b) decentralize procurement of local crops. (e.g., procurement of red rice in Uttarakhand, Jowar in Rajasthan etc.,);
   c) re-evaluate populist measures (loan waivers) due to their limited effectiveness in addressing underlying agricultural distress.

2. a) End to end digitalisation of the process of subsidy allocation can help prevent leakages and corruption, reducing the farm subsidy bill;
   b) rationalisation/targeting of farm subsidies to benefit the small farmers and promote resource efficiency. (e.g., evidence-based delivery of farm subsidy, farm credit);
   c) periodic appraisal of subsidy programs enables evidence-based decisions on their continuation, modification, or phase-out.

3. Improvement of agricultural infrastructure and investing in agriculture research. E.g., every 10-lakh spent on agriculture research brings 328 people out of poverty vs spent on subsidies helps only 26 people (ICRIER study).

There is a need to enhance capital investment and rationalise the subsidy regime in the country. In this light the recommendations of the Kelkar committee and the Standing Committee on Chemicals and Fertilizers can be adopted. (395 words)

## Q9 [Year: 2026]
**Question:** State the objectives and measures of land reforms in India. Also identify the factors responsible for its successful implementation in some parts of the country.

#### Answer from ForumIAS

| **Approach:** • Introduce the answer by giving a brief description of land reforms. <br>• In the body, first state the objectives and measures of land reforms in India. <br>• Next, identify the factors responsible for its successful implementation in some parts of the country. <br>• Conclude by highlighting the recent land reform initiatives undertaken by the government. |
| --- |

Land reforms refer to a series of policy measures taken by the Government in the post-independence era to change the structure of land ownership in India. They sought to address inequalities in land distribution and ensure its transfer from intermediaries to the actual tillers of the land. The objectives and measures of land reforms in India were as follows:
1. a) Dismantling semi-feudal hierarchies, thereby restructuring agrarian power relations ( ‘land to the tiller’ through Zamindari abolition);
   b) removal of institutional and motivational obstacles in the way of agriculture modernization (e.g. absentee landlordism) and a more egalitarian social structure.

2. a) Better utilization of land and labour by incentivizing productive investments in land improvement and providing security of tenure (tenancy reforms);
   b) preventing the concentration of land in the hands of a few to the detriment of others (e.g. land ceiling reforms).

3. a) Economic empowerment of landless farmers, sharecroppers and agricultural labourers (through ownership rights or protected tenancy and enabling land as a collateral for financial services);
   b) mitigating the negative effects of fragmentation of landholdings on agricultural efficiency through state-led consolidation programs.

4. a) Improving the delivery of government support and agriculture extension services to rightful claimants (clearly defined land ownership);
   b) undertaking state-led rural development, enhancing the democratic legitimacy of the post-colonial state.

The measures of land reform include a) zamindari abolition, b) tenancy reforms, c) land ceiling reforms, d) consolidation of landholdings, e) modernization of land records. They were successfully implemented in some parts of the country due to the following factors:
1. a) Abolition of intermediaries was relatively successful as it was undertaken at the Central level (Zamindari was abolished in the 1st  five-year plan);
   b) presence of socially progressive forces (e.g. Kerala, united Andhra Pradesh, West Bengal)  in some regions of the country as opposed to pre-dominance of conservative and landed interests (e.g. Bihar, UP) in the political arena.

2. a) Ideological commitment of the political leadership in some states of the country to the cause of land reforms (e.g. Left Front governments in Kerala and West Bengal, Sheikh Abdullah government in J&K);
   b) history of progressive movements that generated awareness of their rights among peasants (Telangana movement, Tebhaga movement);
   c) dedicated programs for land reforms (Operation Barga in West Bengal for tenancy reforms);
   d) enactment of more progressive and stringent legislation by some states compared to central guidelines (e.g., lower land ceilings in Kerala and West Bengal compared to parts of North India).

3. a) Movements promoting voluntary land transfer (Bhoodan and Gramdan movements);
   b) activities of civil society organizations that put pressure on Governments and political parties (Karshak Sangham Kerala);
   c) presence of politically power groups and new technological interventions (necessary for Green Revolution) to raise agricultural productivity (consolidation reforms relatively succeeded in Punjab, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh).

In recent years, the government's land policy interventions have focused on the correction and computerization of land records, reforming the land survey process, and improving land quality through the reclamation of degraded wasteland and problem soils. SVAMITVA Scheme, Digital India Land Records Programme and Rashtriya Krishi Vikas Yojana (Reclamation of Problem Soils) have been undertaken for the purpose. (522 words)

## Q10 [Year: 2026]
**Question:** Elucidate the importance of buffer stocks for stabilizing agricultural prices in India. What are the challenges associated with the storage of buffer stock? Discuss.
*Metadata: [Year: 2026] [Institute: Forum IAS] [Programme: MGP] [is_pyq: false] [Group: ] [Exam: ] [Stage: Mains] [Paper: Mains - GS 3] [Subject: AGRICULTURE] [Section Group: Agriculture & Farm Dynamics] [Micro Topic: Public Distribution System (PDS) management, buffer stock dynamics, food security missions, and economics of animal rearing] [Sub Topic: Buffer stock & food security] [Macro Tag: Descriptive, Analytical, Applied] [Micro Tag: Elucidate, What are, Discuss, Buffer stocks, India]*
#### Answer from ForumIAS

| **Approach:** • Introduce the answer with a brief background about buffer stock. <br>• In the body, first elucidate the importance of buffer stocks for stabilizing agricultural prices in India. <br>• Next, discuss the challenges associated with the storage of buffer stock. <br>• Conclude by highlighting recent initiative in this regard. |
| --- |

Buffer stock refers to the reserves of food grains or other essential commodities that the government maintains to ensure food security and price stability. In India, it was first introduced during the 4th Five Year Plan (1969-74). The importance of buffer stock in stabilizing agricultural prices in India is discussed below:
1. a) Allows the government to undertake supply side operations to maintain price levels. For e.g., releasing food grains into the market when prices rise and procuring during surplus to prevent a price crash;
   b) constitutes an integral component of PDS, providing subsidized foodgrains to the poor.

2. a) Facilitates mitigating price fluctuations due to seasonality of agricultural production;
   b) helps in addressing any local factors contributing to inflation (artificial scarcity due to hoarding and black marketing, supply disruptions due to natural disasters).

3. a) Helps in countering inflationary pressures, thus protecting the consumers from the effects of the same;
   b) provides an assured market to farmers through direct procurement at MSP, thereby preventing distress sales and cartelization by buyers.

However, the following challenges are associated with the storage of buffer stock:
1. a) Insufficient storage facilities (storage gap of about 35 million tonnes) lead to wastage of grain;
   b) poor storage conditions (open air storage), outdated practices (cover and plinth storage) and lack of modern technologies (temperature control, containerized storage). This increases the spoilage rate (pest infestation, rotting after getting wet in rain);
   c) high operational costs (labour, transport) due to centralized procurement by FCI, NAFED and regional imbalance in storage facilities (concentrated in states like Punjab, Haryana).

2. a) ‘Tied-up capital’ in FCI godowns due to mounting stock of foodgrains, for e.g. FCI holds nearly 300 lakh tonnes of rice (Nov 2024), compared to the required 102.5 lakh tonnes;
   b) market distorting effects of buffer stock (creates artificial demand for products through assured procurement, WTO public stockholding norms);
   c) rice-wheat centricity of buffer stock (over-procurement due to MSP) has ecological and economic consequences.

3. a) Uncertainties in agricultural operation due to climatic factors (heat waves, variability of monsoon) can impact supply for buffer stock maintenance (lowest wheat buffer stock in 7 years after the 2023 procurement season);
   b) excessive government control discourages private sector participation;
   c) allegations of stock diversion and corruption in buffer stock management.

The Government has recently announced an increase in the authorized capital of FCI (from 10000cr to 21000cr). This, along with the pilot project for the world's largest grain storage plan in the cooperative sector, holds the potential to transform buffer stock storage in India. (402 words)
