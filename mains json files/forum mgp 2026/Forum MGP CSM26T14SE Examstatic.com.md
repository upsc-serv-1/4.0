In August 2024, the Union Cabinet approved India's BioE3 Policy (Biotechnology for Economy, Environment, and Employment) as a strategic initiative for fostering high performance (advanced) biomanufacturing. The policy envisages the following key mechanisms to strengthen the biomanufacturing ecosystem in the country:
1. a)Creation of Biomanufacturing Hubs and Biofoundries across India to facilitate the scaling up of production processes and efficient manufacture of bio-based products; b)co-locating these hubs with existing research and infrastructure centers to foster innovation and accelerate the translation and commercialization of biotechnological advancements.

2. a)Integration of AI technologies into biomanufacturing processes through the establishment of Bio-AI Hubs; b)these hubs serve as centers of excellence, fostering interdisciplinary collaboration among academia, industry, and research institutions;
   c) they enable acceleration of data analysis, optimize production processes, and enhance decision-making in areas such as genomics, proteomics, and synthetic biology.

3. a)Introduction of the Bio-RIDE framework, which integrates biotechnology research and entrepreneurship under a cohesive policy structure; b)development of a skilled workforce through developing specialised training programs and higher education programs/curricula, in collaboration with industry leaders.

4. a)Focus on six thematic sectors of national importance viz.
   (i) bio-based chemicals and enzymes, (ii)precision biotherapeutics, (iii)climate resilient agriculture, (iv)functional foods,
   (v) carbon capture and utilization, (vi)futuristic marine and space research);
   b) the policy aims at driving innovation and commercialization in these sectors, which are critical to India's economic and environmental goals.

Overall, the BioE3 policy aligns with the goal of a ‘Net Zero’ economy by 2070 and the vision of the ‘LiFE mission.’ It is expected to  steer India on the path of accelerated ‘Green Growth’ by promoting ‘Circular Bioeconomy’. The Policy will foster sustainability, innovation, and responsiveness to global challenges and lays down the Bio-vision for Viksit Bharat. (270 words)

Dr. Verghese Kurien is considered the key architect behind India’s transformation from a milk-deficit nation into the world’s largest milk producer. He is popularly known as the Father of the White Revolution in India. The role of Dr. Verghese Kurien in revolutionising India’s dairy sector and empowering rural communities:
1. a) Played a pivotal role in transforming the Kaira District Cooperative Milk Producers’ Union Limited (KDCMPUL) into the successful enterprise known as Amul;
   b) infused technology in the supply chain (standard fat checking machine, pasteurization, powder production, cooling and packaging units); c)realised the importance of branding in dairy sector and came out with the name “Amul”(registered in 1957), which has served as a model for cooperatives in several parts of the world.

2. a)Championed and institutionalized the three tier structure- Village societies, District Union and State federations;
   b) established systems ensuring farmers received remunerative prices;
   c) as the founding chairman of the National Dairy Development Board(NDDB) led the replication of the “Anand model” nationwide.

3. a)Launched “Operation Flood” in 1970 under NDDB; b)catapulted India to self-sufficiency and the world’s top milk producer by 1998; c)milk output surged from ~23 MT (1968–69) to ~239 MT (2023-24),per‑capita consumption nearly quadrupled (124gm/day in 1950-51 to 471gm/day in 2023-24).

4. a)Founded Institute of Rural Management Anand(IRMA) in 1979 for cooperative management education and to train rural managers; b)initiated edible-oil cooperative (“Dhara” in 1979) and and the Mother Dairy fruit and vegetable project in Delhi (aimed to provide a direct link between fruit and vegetable growers and consumers).

5. a)Over 85,000 village dairy co-operatives formed inspired from Amul model, empowering 15 million farmers (FAO report on Dairy Development in India); b)promoted inclusion of women in dairy cooperatives, contributing to economic independence and social mobility(e.g.,Mahila Dairy Cooperative Societies).

6. a)Contributed to improved animal health through cooperative veterinary services;
   b) contributed significantly to improved nutrition in rural areas;
   c) pioneered a model of decentralised development through localising production, processing, and profits; d)strengthened social cohesion by breaking caste and religious distinctions.

Dr.Kurien’s main contribution has been as an institution builder,empowering people to develop themselves as he believed that people are the greatest asset of a country. His contribution has earned him many accolades including Padma Vibhushan (1999) and ‘Ramon Magsaysay award (1963). (364 words)

#### Answer from ForumIAS

| **Approach:** • Introduce the answer with a definition of intellectual property rights. <br>• In the body, first highlight the present world scenario of IPR with respect to life materials. <br>• Next, explain the reasons behind the less commercialization of patents in India. <br>• Conclude by highlighting recent government initiatives. |

According to the WTO, Intellectual property rights are the rights given to persons over the creations of their minds. They usually give the creator an exclusive right over the use of his/her creation for a certain period of time. Regarding life-materials, IPR particularly patents are most relevant to inventions in biotechnology, pharmaceuticals and genetic-engineering. The global scenario of IPR with respect to life materials are as follows:
1. a) Adherence to the provisions of international treaty regimes like TRIPS . It mandates minimum IP standards applicable to inventions involving biological materials (GMOs and bio-engineered products);
   b) WIPO’s
   (i) Patent Cooperation Treaty (international patent application),
      (ii) Patent Law treaty (harmonizes formal requirements with respect to patent applications) and
      (iii) Budapest treaty (governing microorganism deposits for patent disclosure) make the global patent system more streamlined.

2. a) The historical dominance of the western countries (US, EU) is being increasingly challenged by emerging economies like India, China (World Intellectual Property Indicators report);
   b) geopolitical instability and uncertain economic outlook as emerging challenges to the global IP ecosystem;
   c) various ethical issues linked to them (patenting of human embryonic cells, excessive commercialization may have adverse impacts on agriculture and health).
   d) various legal concerns (liberal patent regimes like USA vs restrictive regimes like India, EU).

3. a) Societies with large indigenous populations and rich alternative medical systems (India, Brazil) have expressed concerns over biopiracy in developed countries (patents over turmeric, neem);
   b) patent-related disputes (generic medicines) and demands for patent waiver (joint application by India and South Africa for patent waiver on COVID vaccines);
   c) issues of patent cliffs (expiry of patent on a leading item) and consequent efforts for evergreening.

Although India is second in the world to file patents, the following challenges to commercialization of patents persist:
1. a) Inadequate industry-academia linkage results in research that is unsuited to demand dynamics;
   b) the quantity of patents does not necessarily ensure the quality of the same (minor modifications over existing products);
   c) high cost of R&D activities and limited/intermittent access to funding throughout the lifecycle of the project.

2. a) Regulatory barriers (multiple levels of compliance, complexity of documentation and procedure) and long approval time scales (58 months) have resulted in high rates of withdrawal;
   b) shortcomings in the process of granting patents (“arbitrary and mechanical cut paste orders”, allegations of corruption);
   c) weak infrastructure (shortage of manpower in patent office) and licensing policies.

The recent passage of the Anusandhan National Research Foundation Act by the Parliament and other policy initiatives like the National IPR Policy 2016 portend a bright future in the direction of a “Creative India, Innovative India”. (399 words)

Quantum technologies refer to the set of emerging technologies based on the principles of Quantum mechanics (superposition, entanglement, interference, decoherence). Contrary to classical physics, Quantum mechanics studies matter and its interactions with energy on the scale of atomic and subatomic particles.

They can optimize production processes and improve efficiency in the manufacturing sector in the following ways:
1. a) Real time optimization of production-process (scientific resource allocation, staff management, task scheduling, downtime minimization, energy efficiency);
   b) better inventory management; improved maintenance (predictive maintenance through Quantum Machine Learning);
   c) product management (design changes through molecular analysis, quality control through Quantum sensing) to deliver hyper- personalized products.

2. a) Analysis of complex supply chain networks (raw material supply changes, demand fluctuations) through quantum simulation;
   b) determination of transportation routes (real-time routing, path planning);
   c) modelling of financial risks;
   d) allows for contingency planning and gaining a competitive edge over others (detection of market trends);
   e) enhances the scale of innovation in industry;
   f) quantum cryptography enables securing production data and detection of cyber threats in real time.

3. a) Harbinger of Fourth Industrial Revolution by enabling smart manufacturing by integrating emerging technologies;
   b) facilitates incorporation of supercomputing principles (optimization of algorithms);
   c) 

enhances the power of AI in manufacturing (improved data analysis, increased machine learning capability); d) drives the blending of IoT in business operations (secure data transmission).

The deployment of quantum technologies face challenges like de-coherence (hardware instability due to environmental factors), limited scalability of qubits, high costs (both capital and operational) and the lack of a skilled workforce.

Addressing these challenges requires targeted investments in research and development, workforce training and the development of hybrid quantum-classical systems. They can offer more practical, scalable solutions for manufacturing applications. (285 words)

ISRO’s Human Space Flight programme, popularly known as the Gaganyaan mission, aims to send Indian astronauts to Low-Earth Orbit (LEO) for a short duration, onboard an Indian launch vehicle. It is a landmark endeavor for India that aims to achieve two key objectives: demonstration of human spaceflight to LEO (short term) and laying the foundation for a sustained Indian human space exploration programme (e.g. Bhartiya Antriksh Station by 2035, crewed lunar mission by 2040). The programme is expected to have several tangible and intangible benefits for the nation, as discussed below:
1. a) Promotes the development of indigenous technologies, reducing dependence on foreign technology and fostering Atmanirbharta;
   b) consolidates India’s status as a global space power, as it shall join a handful of countries (Russia, USA, China) that have independently developed and executed a human spaceflight mission;
   c) opens new opportunities for space diplomacy through joint exploration projects (NISAR), inter- planetary missions (e.g. Venus Orbiter Mission), and moon mission (Moon Village Association).

2. a) Drives advancement in technology and engineering across multiple sectors (aerospace manufacturing, electronics, materials science);
   b) creation of high-skilled jobs in engineering, manufacturing sectors, provides boost to MSMEs (as vendors for supplying parts);
   c) promotion of STEM education and careers;
   d) fillip to several dual-use technologies (telecommunications, remote sensing, navigation) thus benefiting other sectors as well.

3. a) Enhances the country’s soft power and can be leveraged as a potent foreign policy tool;
   b) acts as inspiration for a new generation of scientists and engineers;
   c) gives template for wider Academia – Industry partnership in taking up development activities;
   d) contributes to the achievement of SDG 9 (Industry, Innovation, Infrastructure) particularly target 9.5 on enhancing scientific research.

However, there are various technical (reliable launch vehicle, astronaut training facilities) and safety challenges (space radiation exposure, space debris) which must be overcome to ensure the success of the mission. The recent Axiom-4 mission with an Indian astronaut aboard (Group Captain Shubhanshu Shukla) marks a critical step in the direction of the final execution of Gaganyaan mission. (333 words)

## Q6 [Year: 2026]
**Question:** What is gene therapy? What makes it a better treatment option than the conventional approaches?
*Metadata: [Year: 2026] [Institute: Forum IAS] [Programme: MGP] [is_pyq: false] [Group: ] [Exam: ] [Stage: Mains] [Paper: GS3] [Subject: SCIENCE & TECHNOLOGY] [Section Group: Everyday Science & Innovations] [Micro Topic: Science and Technology- applications in health, industry, and crisis management] [Sub Topic: Medical and Health Technologies] [Macro Tag: Descriptive, Comparative] [Micro Tag: What is, What makes, Gene therapy, Conventional approaches]*
#### Answer from ForumIAS

| **Approach:** • Introduce the answer by giving a brief explanation about gene therapy. <br>• In the body, discuss its advantages over conventional approaches. <br>• Next, mention the associated concerns and challenges. <br>• Conclude by emphasizing the need for adherence to regulatory framework. |
| --- |

According to the ICMR, Gene Therapy refers to the process of introduction, removal or change in content of an individual’s genetic material to treat a disease and achieve long term cure. It can work by following mechanisms:
1. Replacing a disease-causing gene with a healthy copy of the gene.
2. Inactivating a disease-causing gene that is not functioning properly.
3. Introducing a new or modified gene into the body to help treat a disease.

Advantages of gene therapy over other conventional treatments are as follows:
1. a) Ability to address the root cause of the disease (repairing faulty genes). Conventional approaches usually offer only symptomatic treatment;
   b) greater promise to cure genetic disorders and rare diseases (e.g. OTOF gene therapy for restoring hearing loss).

2. a) Long-lasting benefits, offered by modified genes (e.g. CAR-T Cell therapy uses the body's own immune system to fight cancerous cells). Conventional approaches, however, need regular administration (e.g. insulin injections);
   b) lesser chances of disease relapse as compared to conventional approaches (e.g. cancer recurrence after chemotherapy).

3. a) Improved treatment efficiency (can be targeted to specific cells- e.g. CRISPR CaS 9);
   b) reduced risk of harmful side effects (e.g. hair loss after radiotherapy);
   c) ease of delivery of treatments (e.g. through injections). This can help avoid the complex conventional approaches (e.g. invasive surgeries for brain tumours).

However, it also poses the following risks/challenges:
1. a) Immunological barriers (e.g. rejection of ‘foreign genes’);
   b) risk of unintended mutations (e.g. off target events in CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing) that can pose long-term side effects;
   c) ineffectiveness for complex, multifactorial diseases where the underlying genetic cause is unknown (e.g. Alzheimer's).

2. a) Likely to deepen existing health care divide (prohibitive costs, pharmaceutical monopolies);
   b) concerns due to technological development outpacing regulatory oversight;
   c) ethical considerations arising from artificial genetic selection and human intervention in “godly affairs” (e.g. germline editing to produce designer babies by Chinese biophysicist He Jiankui).

India’s first home-grown gene therapy for cancer was launched at IIT Bombay (April 2024), marking a significant breakthrough in treating complex diseases like cancer. However, continuous advancements under a robust regulatory framework (ICMR's 2019 guidelines), while driving affordability (cell and gene therapy mission mooted by the Principal Scientific Advisor), are essential to address the associated concerns. (382 words)

Artificial intelligence (AI) refers to computer systems capable of performing complex tasks that historically only humans could do, such as reasoning, making decisions, or solving problems. With a potential to contribute $500 billion to the economy (WEF), Artificial Intelligence (AI) serves as a strategic enabler in addressing India's critical socio-economic challenges.

The application of AI can help India in its socio-economic development in the following ways:
1. a) Provides predictive analytics for weather patterns, crop diseases, soil health and market prices, enabling increased agricultural productivity (e.g.,Plantix uses AI-driven image recognition to diagnose crop diseases);
   b) precision farming using AI-driven sensors and drones can boost yield and reduce input costs (e.g., Telangana’s Saagu Baagu initiative, under WEF’s AI4AI program, uses AI to deliver tailored advice to farmers, cutting pesticide use).

2. a) Faster and more accurate diagnoses, personalized treatment plans, and drug discovery for improved healthcare;
   b) enables customized learning experiences, by providing access to quality education resources, and addressing the language barrier (e.g., Bhashini uses AI for real-time translation and speech- to-text in regional languages).

3. a) Improved public service delivery, optimize resource allocation, and enhance transparency in government operations (use in smart traffic management, waste disposal, and public safety);
   b) enable personalized, risk-managed, and automated financial services, enhancing accessibility for all (credit scoring, fraud detection);
   c) boost productivity in manufacturing and services, driving GDP growth, economic development, and job creation (e.g., AI could create 9 million new jobs by 2035-PwC).

However, following hurdles hinder its large-scale adoption:
1. a) Unequal access to digital infrastructure and technology limits AI adoption in certain regions (urban vs rural) and among specific demographics (men vs women);
   b) shortage of skilled AI professionals and limited awareness of AI applications;
   c) challenges in inter-operability with legacy systems.

2. a) Fragmented, outdated and poor quality datasets e.g., limited high-quality regional language data restricts AI use mainly to English and Hindi users.);
   b) data biases, potentially resulting in discriminatory outcomes (e.g., against tribal groups);
   c) absence of a comprehensive regulatory framework around AI ethics and accountability.

3. a) Concerns about privacy breaches and misuse;
   b) difficulties in building public trust (due to fears of job losses, state surveillance);
   c) high energy costs potentially increasing the carbon footprint of the national economy;
   d) issues of data sovereignty and gaps in manufacturing supply chain of digital devices (e.g. semiconductor availability).

Following measures can ensure inclusive and sustainable development as:
1. a) Bridging digital divide and promoting digital literacy through grassroots campaigns (e.g., National Broadband Mission 2.0 to connect villages through OFC, PMGDISHA for enhancing digital literacy);
   b) upskilling the workforce in AI/ML through vocational training and MOOCs (e.g., AICTE-Intel’s AI for Youth, NASSCOM’s FutureSkills Prime);
   c) increasing government and private sector funding for AI research.

2. a) Granting the private sector access to select government databases for commercial use, aligning with the notion of data as a public good (India AI dataset platform, CoEs for AI in different fields);

Nanotechnology is the development of devices and material structures in the size range of 1-100 nanometers (nm) (1 nm is equal to one billionth of a meter). It exploits the unique properties that emerge at this size. It is a transformative, cross-cutting technology with applications spanning, electronics, medicine, agriculture, environmental remediation, and energy storage. Nanotechnology offers innovative solutions to combat pollution and promote environmental sustainability, as discussed below:
1. Air Pollution Control:
   a) Improve the efficiency of industrial filters and scrubbers (e.g. carbon nanotubes);
   b) can accelerate the breakdown of pollutants in the air (wide surface area, alterable chemistry- e.g.

nanocatalysts like TiO2); c) reduce NOx pollution in urban areas (e.g. nano photocatalytic coatings).

2. Water Purification:
   a) Usage in advanced filtration systems (nano-membranes);
   b) can be used to remove tough pollutants (heavy metals, organic pollutants, pathogens);
   c) utility in water purification including desalination and oil spill cleanup.

3. Soil Remediation and waste treatment:
   a) Breakdown agricultural run-off (e.g. pesticides) and industrial pollutants into non-toxic elements (Nanoparticles like zero-valent iron (nZVI));
   b) degrade hazardous substances and reducing the toxicity of industrial waste;
   c) improve efficiency of waste management systems (nano-enabled processes).

4. Customized Pollutant treatment:
   a) Can be modified to selectively target specific contaminants (tailored remediation strategies);
   b) real-time detection of pollutants (quantum dots, metal oxide nanoparticles).

5. Renewable Energy:
   a) Enhance the efficiency of solar cells (e.g., quantum dots, perovskites, nanowires);
   b) improve hydrogen production through water splitting, supporting a green hydrogen economy.

6. Sustainable agriculture:
   a) Release nutrients or active agents in a controlled manner, reducing excess use and runoff (e.g. Nano-fertilizers and nano-pesticides);
   b) promote precision agriculture, minimizing environmental harm while boosting yield (e.g. nano-enabled sensors to capture plant disease, soil moisture status etc).

7. Green Manufacturing:
   a) Reduced raw material usage and extends product life (e.g., lightweight, durable nanocomposites in automotive or construction);
   b) e-waste metal recovery, enabling the sustainable extraction of precious metals;
   c) Nano-enhanced biodegradable materials (like packaging) reduce reliance on plastics and support a circular economy.

While nanotechnology offers advanced solutions for environmental remediation, it faces challenges such as high production costs, potential environmental risks, and regulatory gaps. Addressing these issues requires ongoing research into sustainable practices and the development of robust regulatory frameworks. Initiatives like the ‘Nano Mission’ are steps in the right direction. A Brain-Computer Interface (BCI) is a system that typically facilitates direct communication between the brain and an external device, enabling brain signals to control or influence external activities. It is often aimed at assisting, augmenting or repairing human cognitive or sensory-motor functions. The BCI technology promises transformative applications across domains, as discussed below:
1. a) Applications in healthcare management include aiding diagnosis and monitoring (brain disorders, encephalitis) and treatment for neurological disorders such as Parkinson's disease, epilepsy;
   b) improving quality of life for persons with disability (BCI-controlled prosthetics);
   c) neurofeedback-therapy (conditions like ADHD) and cognitive-rehabilitation for stroke or traumatic brain and spinal cord injury patients.

2. a) Utilization in the education sector [neurofeedback to a student's cognitive state, cognitive- enhancement, skill acquisition, memory-retention improvement (BrainCo’s focus-headband)];
   b) Novel entertainment experiences (mind-controlled video games, neuro-cinema) based on viewer's brain-activity;
   c) scope in areas of art and creativity (neural-networked sculpting, thought-controlled digital art creation or music interfaces, performances using BCI inputs) increases creative experience.

3. a) Usage in neuroergonomics (using neural metrics to analyze mental workload, fatigue) and building smart environments (Samsung Project Pontis);
   b) integration in industrial activities (monitoring operator state for safety) to enhance efficiency and productivity;
   c) faster information processing in communication devices (hands free communication).

4. a) Deployment in the transportation sector (mental control of vehicles for disabled individuals);
   b) intuitive navigation systems controlled by thought (Nissan's Brain-to-Vehicle (B2V) technology);
   c) application in military (situational awareness) and intelligence (confidential communication through DARPA’s Silent talk program).

However, the following risks and challenges are associated with the technology:
1. a) Health risks due to invasive-BCI (infection, bleeding, seizures, and brain-damage), non-invasive BCIs (skin-irritation, headaches, eyestrains) and long-term exposure to electromagnetic-fields; cognitive-fatigue due to high usage;
   b) operational failures (e.g. Neuralink implant’s connective threads retracted from the brain, causing a reduction in the signals the device could capture- May 2024).

2. a) Challenges in feature-extraction and signal processing (difficult to represent the brain signal captured from different electrodes in a single-vector);
   b) operational limitations (age and attention hurdles among children and elderly for data collection);
   c) hardware-limitations (developing compact, portable, and user- friendly BCI-devices).

3. a) Ethical challenges associated with the implementation (obtaining informed consent, behavioural manipulation, privacy concerns, data breaches, etc.);
   b) concerns of brain hacking (capturing brain’s digital-footprints);
   c) could change perceptions of normal human function and exacerbate inequalities (BCI illiteracy);
   d) challenges with respect to developing robust regulatory frameworks, fixing accountability in cases of malfunction or misuse.

Prioritizing improvements in safety and addressing core ethical considerations (data privacy/security, equitable access, preserving cognitive autonomy and agency) is crucial for responsible BCI development and deployment. (386 words)
