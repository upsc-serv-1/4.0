# X-Factor 2026 Ethical Terms Compared

## 1. Ethics vs Morals

[Subject: ETHICS, INTEGRITY & APTITUDE]
[Section Group: Ethics & Human Values]
[Microtopic: Ethics and Human Interface: Essence, Determinants, Consequences, and Dimensions of Ethics]
[Subtopic: Ethics, Morals, Laws, Rules, Conscience]

Ethics and morals both guide human behavior regarding right and wrong, but differ in their source of authority and scope. Ethics represents external social rules, while morals are personal internal standards.

### Aspect Comparison Table

| Aspect | Ethics | Morals |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Definition** | The rules of **conduct recognized** in respect to a particular class of human actions or a particular group or culture. | Principles or habits with respect to **right or wrong** conduct. While morals also prescribe dos and don'ts, morality is ultimately a **personal compass** of right and wrong. |
| **Origin** | **Social system** — **External** | **Individual** - **Internal** |
| **Flexibility** | Ethics tend to be consistent within a **certain context,** but vary between contexts. | Usually consistent, although can change if an **individual**'s beliefs change. |
| **The Gray Area** | A person strictly following **Ethical Principles** may not have any Morals at all. Likewise, one could violate **Ethical Principles** within a given system of rules in order to maintain **Moral integrity**. | A **Moral Person**, although perhaps bound by a **higher covenant**, may choose to follow a **code of ethics** as it would apply to a system. |
| **Origin** | Greek word "**ethos**" meaning "**character**" | Latin word "**mos**" meaning "**custom**" |
| **Acceptability** | Ethics are governed by **professional and legal guidelines** within a particular time and place. | Morality transcends **cultural norms**. |
| **Examples** | **Homosexuality** is considered unethical in some societies while accepted as morally neutral in others. Eating meat is legal and socially ethical in many cultures but considered morally wrong by vegetarians on grounds of personal conscience. | **Adultery** may not be illegal in many countries but is considered deeply immoral by most individuals regardless of legal status. A person may personally consider meat-eating morally wrong even where it is fully legal and socially accepted. |

## 2. Ethics vs Laws

[Subject: ETHICS, INTEGRITY & APTITUDE]
[Section Group: Ethics & Human Values]
[Microtopic: Ethics and Human Interface: Essence, Determinants, Consequences, and Dimensions of Ethics]
[Subtopic: Ethics, Morals, Laws, Rules, Conscience]

Ethics and laws are both frameworks for regulating human behavior. While laws are codified, external rules backed by the state's coercive power, ethics are internal and voluntary, guided by conscience and reason.

### Aspect Comparison Table

| Aspect | Ethics/Morals | Laws |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Nature** | **Internal** moral compass; principles of right and wrong derived from reason and conscience | **External** rules of conduct enforced by the state |
| **Source** | **Individual** reason, social **consensus**, **philosophical traditions** | Legislature, judiciary, executive; codified by sovereign **authority** |
| **Enforceability** | No **formal** enforcement; **compliance** is voluntary and conscience-driven | Enforced through **sanctions, penalties,** imprisonment |
| **Flexibility** | Evolves with **moral reasoning** and social change; contextual | Relatively rigid; changes only through **formal** legislative process |
| **Scope** | **Broader; covers intentions, motives, and character**, not just actions | **Narrower; concerned** only with conduct that can be adjudicated |
| **Conflict Possibility** | An act can be legal but unethical (e.g. tax avoidance, **Volkswagen** emissions fraud) | An act can be illegal but ethical (e.g. **Satyendra Dubey** whistleblowing) |
| **Foundation** | Reason, **virtue**, **duty**, **consequences** | Social contract, **constitutional morality**, **rule of law** |
| **Examples** | Tax avoidance is legal but widely considered unethical. | Section 377 IPC criminalised homosexuality for decades despite ethical arguments for its decriminalisation. |

## 3. Ethics vs Religion

[Subject: ETHICS, INTEGRITY & APTITUDE]
[Section Group: Ethics & Human Values]
[Microtopic: Ethics and Human Interface: Essence, Determinants, Consequences, and Dimensions of Ethics]
[Subtopic: Ethics, Morals, Laws, Rules, Conscience]

Ethics and religion both provide normative guidelines for moral action. Religion derives its authority from divine commands, scriptures, and faith, whereas ethics relies on human reason, rational justification, and philosophical inquiry.

### Aspect Comparison Table

| Aspect | Ethics/Morals | Religion |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Source** | Human **reason,** philosophical inquiry, social experience | Divine command, scripture, religious **authority** |
| **Basis** | **Rational justification;** principles are universally debatable | **Faith-based; accepted** through belief, not necessarily reason |
| **Universality** | Aims to be universal across cultures and beliefs | Particular to **specific** faith traditions; varies across religions |
| **Authority** | **Internal** conscience and rational deliberation | **External** religious **authority**, clergy, scripture |
| **Scope** | Covers all **human conduct** regardless of belief system | Primarily governs conduct of believers within a faith |
| **Overlap** | Most religious traditions contain ethical teachings (ahimsa, **compassion**, justice) | **Religious morality** often aligns with ethical norms but not always |
| **Conflict Possibility** | Ethics can **challenge religious** practices (e.g. caste discrimination justified by religion) | Religion can **inspire ethical** conduct beyond what law requires |
| **Examples** | Caste-based discrimination is widely condemned on ethical grounds of equality and human dignity, even where religious texts have been cited to justify it. Ethical reasoning led to abolition of sati even against religious **custom**. | Dietary prohibitions (beef in Hinduism, pork in Islam) are religiously mandated but carry no ethical weight for those outside the faith. Homosexuality is condemned in several religious traditions but many ethical frameworks affirm its moral neutrality. |

## 4. Law vs Conscience

[Subject: ETHICS, INTEGRITY & APTITUDE]
[Section Group: Ethics & Human Values]
[Microtopic: Ethics and Human Interface: Essence, Determinants, Consequences, and Dimensions of Ethics]
[Subtopic: Ethics, Morals, Laws, Rules, Conscience]

Law represents the codified, external standard established by state authority, while conscience is the internal, subjective moral sense of right and wrong. When laws are unjust, an examined conscience serves as a higher guide.

### Aspect Comparison Table

| Aspect | Law | Conscience |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Nature** | Codified | **external**, public standard of conduct | Internal, subjective moral sense of right and wrong |
| **Source** | State **authority**, **constitutional morality** | **Moral formation** through upbringing, reason, and experience |
| **Enforceability** | Backed by state power and **formal** institutions | Self-enforced; no **external** sanction |
| **Scope** | Covers only **legally cognizable** acts | Covers all **acts including** thoughts, intentions, omissions |
| **Hierarchy** | **Examined conscience** supersedes law when law is unjust | **Examined conscience** > Law > **Unexamined conscience** |
| **Conflict Example** | **Justice H.R. Khanna** dissented in **ADM Jabalpur** even at personal cost | **Aruna Roy** resigned from IAS on grounds **of conscience** |
| **Limitation** | Law can be **technically correct** but substantively wrong (Planning Commission Rs. 32/day poverty line) | **Unexamined conscience** can be biased, parochial, or culturally conditioned |
| **Examples** | Adultery was a criminal offence under Section 497 IPC until 2018 when the Supreme Court struck it down. Homosexuality was criminalised under Section 377 until 2018. | **Justice H.R. Khanna** dissented in **ADM Jabalpur** (1976), upholding the right to life even against a legal emergency order, at the cost of his elevation. |

## 5. Values and Virtues

[Subject: ETHICS, INTEGRITY & APTITUDE]
[Section Group: Ethics & Human Values]
[Microtopic: Human Values: Role of family society and educational institutions in inculcating values]
[Subtopic: Nature of Values]

Values are mental convictions about what is desirable, while virtues are stable, trained habits of character built through practice.

### Aspect Comparison Table

| Aspect | Values | Virtues |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Nature** | **Mental convictions** about what is **important or desirable** | Trained habits of **character** built through **repeated practice over time** |
| **Location** | Live in the mind as **thoughts** and **preferences** | Embedded in **character** as stable dispositions; **the muscle memory of morality** |
| **Moral Direction** | **Morally neutral;** one can value wealth, fame, or independence without becoming **a good person** | Inherently moral; justice, **compassion**, **honesty** carry built-in moral direction. There is no such thing as a bad **virtue** |
| **Stability** | Shift with **environment, age,** and culture. A student values freedom; a parent values security; a corporation shifts values with **market trends** | Stable across **all contexts**. A virtuous person practises **honesty** and **courage** whether a student, a CEO, or in crisis |
| **Source** | Cognitive; arise from beliefs, upbringing, and social environment | Behavioural; formed through habituation, discipline, and deliberate practice (**Aristotle**'s **phronesis**) |
| **Relation to Flourishing** | Necessary but **insufficient; values** state the destination but do not guarantee arrival | Sufficient; **virtue**s are the vehicle and driving skills that actually produce human flourishing |
| **Key Thinkers** | Rokeach (Value Survey); contemporary preference theory | **Aristotle** (Nicomachean Ethics); Alasdair **MacIntyre** (After **Virtue**); W.D. **Ross** |
| **Indian Parallel** | Nishtha — **commitment** to a value or **cause** | Dharma as lived **virtue**; the Gita's sthitaprajna as stable virtuous **character** regardless of circumstance |
| **Examples** | **Valuing health** is a belief. A corporation can state it values sustainability in its mission statement without acting on it. | Practising **temperance** daily to resist overeating. Arjuna's dilemma was resolved not by re-evaluating values but by embodying the **virtue** of **duty**. |

## 6. Deontology vs Utilitarianism vs Virtue Ethics

[Subject: ETHICS, INTEGRITY & APTITUDE]
[Section Group: Governance & Probity]
[Microtopic: Ethics and Human Interface: Essence, Determinants, Consequences, and Dimensions of Ethics]
[Subtopic: Normative Ethics (Deontology vs Teleology)]

These represent the three major branches of normative ethics: Deontology (rule-based), Teleology/Utilitarianism (consequence-based), and Virtue Ethics (character-based).

### Aspect Comparison Table

| Aspect | Deontology | Teleology | Virtue Ethics |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Definition** | An ethical theory that emphasizes the importance of following rules, duties, and **obligation**s. | An ethical theory that focuses on the outcomes or **consequences** of actions, aiming to maximize overall happiness and minimize **suffering**. | An ethical theory that emphasizes the role of **character** and **virtue** in moral philosophy. |
| **Key Principles** | **Duty**, **obligation**, adherence to **moral rules**. | **Greatest happiness** principle, **utility**, **consequences**. | **Virtue**s, moral **character**, **practical wisdom** (**phronesis**). |
| **Nature** | **Rule-based** and **absolutist**. | **Consequence-based** and **relativist**. | **Character**-based and **agent-centered**. |
| **Strengths** | Provides clear guidelines and **consistency** in moral decision-making. | Flexibility in considering the context and **consequences**, aiming for overall well-being. | Focuses on **personal development** and moral growth, promoting a holistic view of ethics. |
| **Weaknesses** | Can be rigid and ignore the **consequences** of actions. | Can **justify morally** questionable actions if they produce good outcomes. | Lacks clear guidelines for action in **specific** situations. Relying on **individual** judgment. |
| **Key Scholars and Theories** | Immanuel **Kant**: **Categorical Imperative**; W.D. Ross: **Prima Facie Duties** | Jeremy Bentham: Classical **Utilitarianism**; John Stuart Mill: Rule **Utilitarianism** | Aristotle: Nicomachean Ethics; Alasdair MacIntyre: After **Virtue** |
| **Real-Life Examples** | **Gandhi**ji withdrawing **NCM** after **Chauri Chaura**; **Professional Ethics**: A doctor refusing to perform **euthanasia** because it is against medical **ethical code**s. | Business Decisions: A company laying off a small portion of its workforce to save the majority; **Medical Triage**: Prioritizing treatment for those with the highest chances of survival in disaster scenarios. | Mahatma **Gandhi**: Emphasis on **virtue**s like non-violence and truth in his struggle for Indian independence; **Modern Education**: **Character education** teaching **virtue**s like **honesty**, respect, and **responsibility**. |

## 7. Ethical Relativism vs Ethical Absolutism

[Subject: ETHICS, INTEGRITY & APTITUDE]
[Section Group: Ethics & Human Values]
[Microtopic: Ethics and Human Interface: Essence, Determinants, Consequences, and Dimensions of Ethics]
[Subtopic: Term Based]

Ethical relativism asserts that morals depend on cultural or individual contexts, whereas ethical absolutism holds that universal moral standards apply to everyone.

### Aspect Comparison Table

| Aspect | Ethical Relativism | Ethical Absolutism |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Definition** | The belief that morality is relative to the norms of one's culture or **individual** perspective, meaning there are no **universal moral standards**. | The belief that there are **universal moral standards** that apply to all people, regardless of **culture** or **individual** beliefs. |
| **Nature** | Contextual and subjective, varying with cultural or **individual** norms. | Fixed and objective, based on **universal principles**. |
| **Key Principles** | **Cultural sensitivity**, **tolerance**, and **acceptance of diversity in moral beliefs**. | Universality, **objectivity**, **consistency**. |
| **Strengths** | Promotes understanding and **tolerance** of different moral perspectives and practices. | Provides clear and consistent guidelines for moral behavior, ensuring **fairness** and **justice**. |
| **Weaknesses** | Can lead to **moral ambiguity** and the inability to critique harmful practices. | Can be rigid and intolerant of cultural diversity, potentially leading to **moral imperialism**. |
| **Examples** | **Death penalty**, acceptable in some cultures but not in others. | Universal condemnation of practices like **slavery**, **genocide**, and **torture**; **Universal Declaration of Human Rights**. |

## 8. Moral Intuition vs Moral Reasoning

[Subject: ETHICS, INTEGRITY & APTITUDE]
[Section Group: Ethics & Human Values]
[Microtopic: Ethics and Human Interface: Essence, Determinants, Consequences, and Dimensions of Ethics]
[Subtopic: Ethics, Morals, Laws, Rules, Conscience]

Moral intuition is the immediate, automatic response to an ethical situation, while moral reasoning is the slow, deliberate process of logical reflection.

### Aspect Comparison Table

| Aspect | Moral Intuition | Moral Reasoning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Definition** | **Immediate, automatic** ethical judgments without conscious **deliberation** | Deliberate, reflective process of evaluating **ethical principles** and outcomes based on moral principles, potential **consequences**, and logical considerations. |
| **Process** | Instinctive, often subconscious | Conscious, analytical, and reflective |
| **Basis** | Emotions, gut feelings, **and** instinct | Logical analysis, ethical theories, and principles |
| **Speed** | Quick, instantaneous | Slower, requiring time and effort |
| **Source** | Evolutionary psychology, cultural and social conditioning | Education, rational thought, philosophical frameworks |
| **Flexibility** | Less flexible as it is deeply ingrained and **reactive**. | More **flexible, allows** for reconsideration and adjustment based on new information |
| **Advantages** | Quick, efficient, often aligns with **social norms** | Thorough, can **handle complex** situations, **justifiable** |
| **Disadvantages** | Prone to bias, may lack **consistency** or **thoroughness** | Time-consuming, can be **overly complex** or detached from real-life nuances |
| **Examples** | Witnessing an act of **theft might** instantly trigger a feeling that it's wrong. | Understanding the **entire context** behind the theft. |

## 9. Social Values vs Economic Values

[Subject: ETHICS, INTEGRITY & APTITUDE]
[Section Group: Ethics & Human Values]
[Microtopic: Human Values: Role of family society and educational institutions in inculcating values]
[Subtopic: Nature of Values]

Social values prioritize community welfare, justice, and human dignity, whereas economic values focus on financial value, efficiency, and resource optimization.

### Aspect Comparison Table

| Aspect | Social Values | Economic Values |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Definition** | **Social values** refer to the moral standards and principles that govern behaviors within a society. | **Economic values** are quantifiable measures related to the cost, benefits, and economic worth of goods and services. |
| **Primary Goal** | Promoting a **harmonious, equitable,** and just society. | Maximizing economic efficiency, growth, and wealth creation |
| **Key Elements** | Trust, cooperation, **fairness**, justice, community, and respect | Cost, revenue, profit, market value, and economic efficiency |
| **Scope** | Broader, encompassing various aspects of life beyond economic transactions | Narrower, primarily focused on financial and economic activities |
| **Measurement** | **Qualitative**, often assessed through **cultural indicators** and social research | **Quantitative**, measured through financial **metrics** |
| **Influence** | Driven by **cultural, historical,** and societal **factors** | Driven by **market forces**, economic policies, and financial incentives |
| **Adaptability** | Evolves with changes in **societal norms** and cultural shifts | Adjusts based on **market conditions** and economic policies |
| **Potential Conflicts** | May conflict with **economic values** when ethical or societal **obligation**s lead to less economically efficient outcomes. | May conflict with **social values** when maximizing profit or efficiency undermines social welfare or ethical standards. |
| **Interrelation** | **Social values** can influence economic policies (e.g., **ethical business practices**) | **Economic values** can impact social conditions (e.g., **wealth inequality**) |
| **Policy Implications** | Inform social policies like healthcare, education, and welfare | Inform economic policies like taxation, trade, and monetary policy |
| **Examples** | Equality, freedom, human rights, **environmental sustainability** | Cost-benefit analysis, **return on investment**, supply and demand |

## 10. Role of Family and Society

[Subject: ETHICS, INTEGRITY & APTITUDE]
[Section Group: Ethics & Human Values]
[Microtopic: Human Values: Role of family society and educational institutions in inculcating values]
[Subtopic: Nature of Values]

Both family and society act as vital institutions for value-inculcation, adapting their roles across different stages of an individual's lifespan.

### Aspect Comparison Table

| Life Stage | Role of Family | Role of Society |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Infancy (0-2 years)** | - **Provide love,** care, and security: A mother soothes her crying baby to create a sense of safety.
- Introduce basic social norms: Parents teach the baby to say "please" and "thank you" through repetition. | - Support through **community care** systems: A neighbourhood playgroup allows babies to interact in a safe environment.
- Reinforce family values through social interactions: Neighbours smile and greet the baby, reinforcing friendly behaviour. |
| **Early Childhood (3-6 years)** | - Teach basic manners and respect for others: A father **encourage**s his child to share toys with siblings.
- Model positive behaviour and communication: Parents resolve conflicts calmly in front of their child. | - **Foster social** interactions in safe environments: A community playground where children learn to play together.
- Introduce **cultural norms** through festivals and community events: Attending a local Diwali celebration to learn **cultural traditions**. |
| **Middle Childhood (7-12 years)** | - **Encourage** **responsibility** and **accountability**: Parents assign chores, like setting the table, to teach **responsibility**.
- Support educational development and curiosity: A mother reads bedtime stories to stimulate imagination. | - Provide platforms for group activities and **teamwork**: A local football team teaches **teamwork** and cooperation.
- Reinforce societal norms and ethical behaviour through communal activities: Scouts programs that promote **honesty** and community service. |
| **Adolescence (13-18 years)** | - Guide moral and ethical decision-making: Parents discuss the importance of **honesty** when their teen faces **peer pressure**.
- Offer emotional support and understanding during identity formation: A father listens without judgment to his teen's concerns. | - Promote **civic responsibility** and **community engagement**: Teenagers volunteer in community clean-up drives organised by local groups.
- Provide **role model**s and mentors through social organisations: Youth **mentorship** programs offering guidance from positive adult **role model**s. |
| **Young Adulthood (19-29 years)** | - **Encourage** independence and self-reliance: Parents support their child's decision to move out for university.
- Support career choices and personal goals: Family members celebrate job promotions and career milestones. | - **Foster professional** networking and community involvement: Professional associations and clubs facilitate career networking.
- Reinforce societal values through workplace norms and public service opportunities: Companies promote ethical practices and community service. |
| **Adulthood (30-59 years)** | - **Provide ongoing** support and advice: Parents advise their adult children on parenting their own kids.
- Model **balance**d life values: A mother manages work-life **balance**, demonstrating how to juggle responsibilities. | - **Encourage** **participation** in community services and local governance: Adults engage in local school boards or community councils.
- Maintain **cultural traditions** and societal responsibilities: Communities uphold cultural festivals and public service traditions. |
| **Late Adulthood (60+ years)** | - Offer **wisdom** and experience to younger generations: Grandparents share stories of their youth with grandchildren.
- Provide emotional support and care in old age: Family members ensure their elderly parents are well-cared-for and included in family activities. | - Respect and **honour elderly** through social norms and community practices: Societies celebrate the elderly during special senior citizen days. |

## 11. Social Influence vs Persuasion

[Subject: ETHICS, INTEGRITY & APTITUDE]
[Section Group: Psychology & Foundational Values]
[Microtopic: Attitude: Content, Structure, Function, Moral and Political Attitudes, Social Influence]
[Subtopic: Social influence and persuasion]

Social influence involves the structural and peer forces that alter individual behavior, whereas persuasion represents target-oriented, rational, or emotional communication.

### Aspect Comparison Table

| Aspect | Social Influence | Persuasion | Overlap |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Definition** | The process by which **individual**s change their thoughts, feelings, or behaviors due to the presence or actions of others. | The act of **convincing someone** to change their belief or behavior through argument, reasoning, or appeal. | Both involve changing an **individual**'s thoughts, feelings, or behaviors. |
| **Methods** | **Conformity**, **compliance**, **obedience**, **socialization**, **peer pressure**. | **Logical argument**, **emotional appeal**, **credibility**, evidence. | Both can use similar techniques such as appeals to emotions or **authority**. |
| **Agents** | Groups, peers, **authority** figures, **cultural norms**. | **Individual**s, marketers, politicians, speakers. | Both can be influenced by **respected figures** or authorities. |
| **Goals** | Often **implicit, aimed** at group harmony or social norms. | Explicit, aimed at **specific** outcomes or behaviors. | Both aim to **change behavior** or attitudes. |
| **Contexts** | Social settings, peer groups, cultural environments. | Marketing, politics, personal interactions. | Can occur in **various contexts** such as work, school, or public spaces. |
| **Theoretical Basis** | Social psychology, sociology, cultural studies. | Communication theory, rhetoric, psychology. | Both draw from psychological principles. |
| **Resistance Factors** | Strong personal values, awareness of influence tactics. | Critical thinking, counter-arguments, prior beliefs. | Resistance can occur due to strong pre-existing attitudes. |
| **Outcome Focus** | Group cohesion, adherence to social norms. | Change in **specific** beliefs or actions. | Both result in some form of change in the **target audience**. |
| **Examples** | **Swachh Bharat** Abhiyan used social influence through community norms and peer behaviour to change sanitation habits. Untouchability was sustained for centuries through social influence and fear of ostracism. | **Gandhi**'s non-cooperation movement used moral persuasion to convince millions to abandon British institutions. B.R. **Ambedkar** persuaded the Constituent Assembly through rational argument to enshrine fundamental rights. | Both social influence and persuasion were used in the anti-dowry movement: community norm shifts (influence) and legal advocacy through **logical argument** (persuasion) together drove change. |

## 12. Bureaucratic Attitude vs Democratic Attitude

[Subject: ETHICS, INTEGRITY & APTITUDE]
[Section Group: Psychology & Foundational Values]
[Microtopic: Attitude: Content, Structure, Function, Moral and Political Attitudes, Social Influence]
[Subtopic: Attitude for Civil Servants]

The bureaucratic attitude emphasizes rules, hierarchy, and formality, while the democratic attitude stresses participation, consensus, and flat delegation.

### Aspect Comparison Table

| Aspect | Bureaucratic Attitude | Democratic Attitude | Overlap |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Definition** | A mindset focused on adherence to rules, **hierarchy**, and **formal procedures**. | A mindset focused on **participation**, equality, and **majority rule**. | Both are approaches to governance and organizational **management**. |
| **Decision-Making** | **Top-down**, decisions made by higher authorities. | **Bottom-up**, decisions made through **collective input** and voting. | Both aim to achieve efficient and effective **management**. |
| **Structure** | Hierarchical, clear **chain of command**. | Flat, with **power distributed** among members. | Both require some form of structure to **function effectively**. |
| **Flexibility** | Rigid, with **strict adherence** to rules and procedures. | Flexible, open to discussion and change based on **consensus**. | Both may need to **balance** stability and adaptability. |
| **Focus** | Efficiency, order, and **control**. | Inclusivity, **fairness**, and representation. | Both aim to **achieve goals** and maintain order within an organization or society. |
| **Communication** | **Formal**, through official channels and documentation. | In**formal**, open dialogues and discussions. | Both **require clear** communication to function effectively. |

| Aspect | Code of Ethics | Code of Conduct |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Definition** | A set of principles and values that guide ethical decision-making. Eg. Promoting **integrity**, **transparency**, **responsibility**. | **Specific guidelines** that dictate the expected behaviors of **individual**s. Eg. No gifts from clients, adherence to **specific** dress codes, punctuality. |
| **Focus** | Broad **ethical principles** and organizational values. | **Specific** rules and actions that are to be followed or avoided. |
| **Nature** | More **general** and abstract. | More **specific** and detailed. |
| **Purpose** | To provide a framework for **ethical judgment** and decision-making. | To clearly state what is allowed and what is not in **specific** contexts. |
| **Application** | Serves as a **moral compass** for overall conduct. | Serves as a practical guide for actions in **specific** situations. |
| **Flexibility** | Usually more **flexible, open** to interpretation. | Typically more rigid and prescriptive. |
| **Enforcement** | Less about enforcement, more about guiding values. | Often accompanied by **disciplinary** measures for non-**compliance**. |
| **Examples** | Promoting **integrity**, **transparency**, **responsibility**; **2nd ARC** recommendation of **Code of Ethics** | No gifts from clients, adherence to **specific** dress codes, punctuality. |
